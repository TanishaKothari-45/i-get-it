# Learning to see: notice, then name

Before your AI can build what you want, you have to be able to say it. This chapter trains the two skills under design taste, noticing the small things and naming them exactly, on one real page.

> **By the end of this chapter you'll have**
>
> **A teardown of one page.** Ten things you noticed on one page you admire, each written as an element and what it does, plus the one or two you want in your own product.
>
> Example **Page:** Shopify's Renaissance Edition. **Two of the ten:** the one AI input has a soft gradient, so it reads as AI on a classical page · cards rise from behind at about 70 degrees, growing as they come. **For PhotoCal:** the one AI moment, “Reading your plate…”, gets a treatment of its own.

## You can already tell a good page from a bad one

Open two apps on your phone, one you love and one you put up with, and you'll know within a second which is which.

The trouble starts when you try to say why. It's not that people don't understand what a good website is. Very smart people can't describe what is happening, so they say it's clean, it's minimal, it's more classy, it's more polished.

None of those words tells anyone how to build the page, and your AI can only build what you can say.

Most people have not had to build design taste in a very focused way. It can be built. A lot of articles will make you feel good about how to build taste: travel, be part of experiences. That's not how it works. Everyone gets something different from travel; some people feel fulfilled inside and still can't describe that taste outward. And just because you can buy expensive things doesn't mean you have taste. Think of the people with the big Louis Vuitton belt. **Money can't buy taste.**

The first level is to have taste. The second is to know what kind of taste you have. Your taste can be different from mine, and that's completely fine. But a lot of people use that to say they don't need taste at all. That isn't true. There are some fundamental truths that everyone knows don't work.

> **Remember this**
>
> Taste, for a builder, is two skills you can practise: **care**, noticing the small things, and **label**, naming them exactly.

## Care: scroll slower than feels normal

Care means caring about, and noticing, the small things. Practise it: open a page you like, refresh it and scroll very slowly. Keep noting down everything you notice, even the smallest of things.

It's harder than it sounds, for a reason. Tony Fadell, who led the team behind the iPod, [calls it habituation (opens in a new tab)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9uOMectkCCs&t=170s): our brains turn everyday things into habits so we can stop noticing them. Scrolling slowly switches that off.

Keep talking while you do it, even if nobody can hear you. Imagine that you're explaining it to yourself. The moment you can't find a word is the moment you've found something to learn.

Start on a page where a lot is happening. There are so many things going on that it's very hard not to notice. On a subtle page you'd notice almost nothing, or only the letter spacing.

Take that effort in the beginning. Then keep training your eye with a tiny diary: [one widely shared post (opens in a new tab)](https://x.com/squarehaunting/status/2098410811383574677) suggests writing yes or no to whether you liked something, plus a few words on why; around a hundred entries in, you notice the change.

For a while you'll see what's wrong with your own screens before you can fix it. The radio host Ira Glass called it the gap between your taste and your work, and [designers still point to it (opens in a new tab)](https://x.com/rsms/status/1634683866773618688): only volume closes it.

## Label: name the element, then what it does

Your AI can't see what you noticed. It only gets your words.

I noticed something is wrong. How do I describe it? Is it blurry? Is it offset? Jargon exists to make things efficient: when I say MVP, minimum viable product, I don't explain the concept again and again. Know the right words and your life gets efficient too. No one's going to tell you this.

Here's the part almost everyone gets wrong at first. When people describe a page, they describe what it does to them: it feels alive, it pulls you in. That's the **outcome**, the effect on you. Your AI can't build an effect. It can only build the thing on the screen that caused it, the **element**.

So list elements. I see an element: can I describe what that element is doing, exactly? I like the skateboard because of the shine and the shadow on it. It's not flat. That's what I want you to start noticing.

“This element shined like a blast, or a paper burn.” That's great labelling.

A good label usually has three parts: **which element**, **what it does**, and **how** (the direction, the speed, the amount). Each part you leave out is a decision your AI makes for you, and it will make the most common one.

- **Try 1Fits every website ever made**: It looks cleanNot yet
- **Try 2An outcome: what it does to you, not what's on the screen**: It feels aliveNot yet
- **Try 3An element, but no direction, speed or amount**: The cards animate nicelyNot yet
- **PassesElement, what it does and how: your AI can build this**: Each card rises from behind the one before it, at about 70 degrees, growing as it comesPasses

*Figure: Every step down the ladder removes one guess your AI would otherwise make.*

Vague design words are the same mistake as a vague user. “Everyone” and “clean” fail for the same reason: they rule nothing out, so they decide nothing. The same goes for what's off: “this is ugly” tells your AI nothing. Describe the pain in detail instead, like: “I can't read a spreadsheet when cells use different fonts, aren't centre aligned or the text isn't wrapped.”

Here's what labels look like on one ordinary landing page:

1. NavigationLogo left, small and grey, so it never competes with the headline.
2. HeadlineTwo lines, the biggest text, broken by hand so no line is one word.
3. Line under itOne grey sentence, a third of the headline's size.
4. ButtonFull width, near the bottom, the only coloured thing on the screen.
5. ImageA plate shot from above in warm light, its rim running off the edge.
6. CardSits on the photo with a soft shadow, rising a little as it fades in.

*Figure: Name the part, then what it does. Six labels like these tell your AI more than “make it clean” ever will.*

**▸ A starter vocabulary — Seven words for what you see on a still screen**

These words cover most of what you'll notice on a still screen. Learn each by finding it on a page you like.

- **Spacing**: The empty space between things. A small gap reads as one group; a bigger gap, a new section.
- **Alignment**: What lines up with what. Text that almost lines up looks like a mistake.
- **Size and weight**: How big, and how thick, text is. Bold can make something important without making it bigger.
- **Letter spacing**: The space between letters, also called tracking. Tighter makes a headline feel denser.
- **Contrast**: How different text is from its background. Too little and it can't be read.
- **Radius**: How round the corners are. Sharp feels formal, round feels friendly, a mix looks accidental.
- **Shadow and gradient**: A shadow lifts something up; a gradient fades one colour into another.

**▸ Common mistakes — Three ways a teardown goes wrong**

- **Describing the message, not the page**: “They want you to focus on the AI” is your reading. Write down what's on the screen that made you think it.
- **Words that fit any screen**: “Good white space and information hierarchy” fits a thousand sites. If a word would fit another site unchanged, it isn't a label yet.
- **Starting on a subtle page**: The differences are a few percent. Warm up on a busy page first.

> **[Advanced]**
>
> ### Advanced Motion words: describing how things move
>
> Most of what makes a page feel alive is motion, and motion has its own vocabulary. Without it you're left with “it animates nicely”, which your AI will translate into the most common animation there is: everything fading in.
>
> I liked how this element came in: it zoomed in and came, instead of fading in. That one sentence already uses two of the words below. Six of them cover nearly everything.
>
> - **Entrance**: How it arrives: a **fade** (from invisible), a **slide** (from somewhere else), a **scale** or zoom (from smaller or bigger), or a **reveal** (uncovered, as if a mask is pulled away).
> - **Origin and direction**: Where it comes from: below, behind, the left, the centre. “Cards come from behind at about 70 degrees” is an origin and a direction.
> - **Duration**: How long it takes. Per [NN/g (opens in a new tab)](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/animation-duration/) (the Nielsen Norman Group, a usability research firm), most interface animations should take 100 to 500 milliseconds, at 500 they start to feel like a drag, and something like a panel moving into view suits 200 to 300.
> - **Easing**: How the speed changes along the way. [Ease-out (opens in a new tab)](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/CSS/easing-function) starts fast and slows towards the end, which is why it suits things arriving. Linear, the same speed throughout, tends to look mechanical.
> - **Stagger**: Several things arriving one after another rather than all at once, a short delay apart, so the eye reads them in order.
> - **Trigger**: What starts it: the page loading, a scroll, a tap. Hover doesn't exist on a phone, so nothing important should depend on it.
>
> Put together, a motion label for PhotoCal sounds like this: *when the answer arrives, the result card fades in and rises a few pixels over about 250 milliseconds, easing out, and the calorie number counts up from zero over the same time.* An agent can build that sentence exactly. It can't build “make the result feel satisfying”.
>
> Motion costs attention. Each moving thing pulls the eye, so two things moving at once compete, and motion on every element means none of it stands out. Some people also feel unwell with a lot of motion, which is why phones have a reduce-motion setting that a web page [can detect (opens in a new tab)](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/CSS/@media/prefers-reduced-motion). Tell your agent to respect it: keep the fades, drop the big movements.

## One page, ten labels: Shopify's Renaissance Edition

Here's the practice done once, on the page GrowthX's Build Week look came from. Open [Shopify's Renaissance Edition (opens in a new tab)](https://www.shopify.com/editions/winter2026), refresh it, scroll very slowly and write your own list before you read this one.

Ten things worth noticing:

- **The load**: The experience starts the moment the page loads. AI sits in the centre, a square draws in around it, and then everything zooms out. They make you focus on the centre, in a creative way.
- **Two worlds**: Two layers move separately. That's not just design, it's the concept: Renaissance figures wearing sneakers, carrying shopping bags and Starbucks-like cups. Even the one serif word in the headline merges two worlds. You see it again and again.
- **Low readability**: At first you can only read “Renaissance Edition”. That's a feature: the rest is what the business team needed on the page, and that one line is what they want to talk about.
- **3D to 2D**: The skateboard is 3D until you scroll, then it goes 2D, with a shine.
- **The touch**: When the fingers touch, a light goes off like a blast and expands, and the centre, then the left and right, slowly lose colour to black and white. The next person emerges in the same style, looks into your eye and blinks.
- **Paper burn**: The next text comes in with the same blast, like paper burning, but in reverse.
- **Magazine**: Page 1, 2, 3 in the lower left, in italics. The design is a website, but the fonts are a magazine.
- **Soft gradient**: The design is classical, but the input for Sidekick, Shopify's AI assistant, has a soft gradient, to tell you it's an AI feature. Very intentional.
- **From behind**: Cards come out from behind at about 70 degrees, growing as they rise. That's why it feels real.
- **Texture**: The texture on the hand is like cracks in a desert land. Cotton against silk.

Let me be very honest with you: a user doesn't spend this much time on the website, but they leave thinking it was a great website. It's the small things adding up.

And the touch is very specific: it's pure code. The website was made with AI and people together. The AI image took someone caring about the small things to describe it and direct it.

That last word is the whole lens. Someone noticed, named and directed, and the crew built it. And because an interaction like the touch is code, your crew can build one too.

> **[Advanced]**
>
> ### Advanced The subtle page: what a careful copy still missed
>
> Once a busy page feels easy, try a quiet one. This is where taste gets precise, because nothing moves and every difference is small.
>
> Someone copied the GrowthX Chrome extension, and copied it badly. The font is very similar. Here's what changed, and what they missed.
>
> - **Contrast**: Our colour is brighter and the contrast is better; theirs isn't a deep black. The blacks aren't black enough.
> - **Letter spacing**: Ours is custom, minus 4%. And on our extension the letter spacing and font size change with your viewport, the width of your screen.
> - **Centring**: Their main text looks off-centre. Actually it is centred; it's just the letter spacing that's making it feel like that.
> - **Line breaks**: A custom line break, so the headline looks pretty, not wherever the text happens to wrap.
> - **Font pairing**: They used DM Mono, and it doesn't go with this.
>
> This is the difference between something that looks polished and something that looks not so polished: caring about the small things.
>
> One more difference shows up side by side: the original has four small elements in its corners, so it feels framed.
>
> Notice what kind of list that is. Not one item would show up in a screenshot at a glance, and not one of them is a matter of opinion. Each is measurable: a colour value, a percentage, a position, a line break. That's what makes a subtle page such good training, once you're ready for it. It forces you past “theirs feels cheaper” to the exact property that differs.
>
> A method that works on any quiet page:
>
> - **1**: Same size · Put two screenshots side by side · The page you admire and one that looks a bit off, at the same width. Flip between them rather than staring at one.
> - **2**: One property · Compare one thing at a time · Contrast first, then spacing, then letter spacing, then alignment, then line breaks. Looking for everything at once finds nothing.
> - **3**: Measure · Check what you suspect · In Chrome, right-click any text and choose Inspect: the panel shows its exact size, weight, letter spacing and colour. Your eye finds the difference; the inspector tells you its name and number.
> - **4**: Write it · As a label you could hand over · “Headline letter spacing minus 4%, deep black on white, broken by hand after the third word.”
>
> *Figure: A quiet page, taken apart one property at a time.*
