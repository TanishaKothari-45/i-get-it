# The job: what people hire your product to do

Before flows or screens, you need one sentence: the progress your user wants, at the moment it hurts. This chapter gets you that sentence.

> **By the end of this chapter you'll have**
>
> **The job, in one sentence.** One sentence that says when it hurts, what they want right then, and the one thing it lets them do. Plus who your user is by situation, and what they do about it today.
>
> Example When my lunch arrives and I'm trying to lose weight, I want to know roughly what I'm eating without stopping to log it, so I can stay under my number without giving up food I like.

## The job is the progress, not the product

Picture two food delivery apps.

**A polished app that misses the job**
**Smooth, well built, absolutely amazing,** and the food arrives cold and late.

**A rough app that does the job**
**Slightly buggy,** and the food comes fast and very hot. You use this one.

*Figure: The job is satisfying your hunger. Polish doesn't count if the job doesn't get done.*

That's a **job to be done**: the progress someone is trying to make, at a particular moment in their life. Your product is only what they hire to make it.

> **Remember this**
>
> People don't want your product. They want the progress, and your product is what they hire to make it.

> **[Advanced]**
>
> ### Advanced A job has three sides
>
> A job has three sides: what needs doing, how they want to feel while it's done, and how they want to look to others.
>
> - **What needs doing**: Know roughly how many calories are on the plate.
> - **How he wants to feel**: In control, not guilty. Not lectured about rice.
> - **How he wants to look**: Not like the person weighing his dal at the office lunch table.
>
> The second and third lines are why a calorie app with a perfect database still gets quit: it does the practical job and fails the other two. Write all three when the feeling or the look is a big part of why people choose.

## Write it as one sentence

A job sentence has three parts:

> **The job story**
>
> **When** [the moment it hurts], **I want to** [what they want right then], **so I can** [the one thing it lets them do].

- **1**: When · My lunch arrives and I'm trying to lose weight · A moment you could watch happen: a plate on a desk, 1:15 PM on a Tuesday.
- **2**: I want to · Know roughly what I'm eating without stopping to log it · What he wants right then. Logging one meal in an app takes about three minutes, so he eats instead.
- **3**: So I can · Stay under my number without giving up food I like · The progress, in his words.

*Figure: PhotoCal's job. Not one word of it mentions an app, a photo or AI.*

Now the test. Take your product out of the sentence. If it still makes sense, you've written a job. If it falls apart, you've written your product.

Keep one “so I can”. If you write several, you're listing tasks, and tasks turn into features. A product has two or three jobs at most; write the one you'll build for this weekend.

## The hard part: a job, not a solution

Most people come with a solution in mind and fit it into the format. Here is the same idea, written four times.

- **Try 1The product, wearing a job's clothes**: I want an AI app that counts calories from a photo of my foodNot yet
- **Try 2Too broad to decide anything**: I want to be healthierNot yet
- **Try 3A task inside a tool he's already quit**: I want to track my caloriesNot yet
- **PassesThe job**: When my lunch arrives and I'm trying to lose weight, I want to know roughly what I'm eating without stopping to log it, so I can stay under my number without giving up food I likePasses

*Figure: Each line moves closer to the moment, and further from the product.*

A job can have more than one moment. Lunch at a desk is one; reading a menu on holiday is another. Note three to five of these moments per user; your sentence uses the most common one.

## Ask what they did, not what they will do

This sentence comes from real people, not from your head. If you've spoken to your users, you come with good clarity. A lot of people haven't. Talk to one or two.

One rule: never ask people what they're going to do. Ask what they did. Past behaviour is the best predictor of future behaviour.

- **Ask**: “Tell me about the last time this happened. What did you do about it?” There's always something, even if it's a WhatsApp group.
- **Ask**: “What happens when it doesn't get done?” That's how you find out how much it hurts.
- **Look**: Ask to see what they actually did: their chats, their notes. What people say and what they do are often different.
- **Don't ask**: “Would you use an app that…?” Almost everyone says yes.

Then imagine you're a new hire, and your user is the boss who hired you for this job. What would you ask? What does “roughly” mean to Rohan: within 50 calories, or 200? Ask at the level of the job, not the technology.

And your product is not going to change behaviour. It can make people realise something, but changing is on them.

## Your user is a situation, not a demographic

Age, city, title: none of it matters. The same person behaves differently in a different city, or a different company.

Describe your user by their situation instead. Not “28, Bengaluru”, but: someone trying to lose weight who has quit a calorie app twice, because logging one meal takes about three minutes. That's Rohan, PhotoCal's user in every chapter from here. For a business, describe its situation the same way: a firm of one to five partners, not a city or a sector.

If your product has more than one person in it, like an Airbnb host and their housekeeping staff, write a job for each. Then build for one of them this weekend; the other appears as steps inside that person's flow.

**▸ In B2B, a bench hires you — A CRM for legal counsels, narrowed in four questions**

When a business buys software, one person rarely decides. Here's how a broad idea, a CRM for legal counsels, narrows once you ask the job.

- **1**: The user · Who exactly? · Legal counsels have very different jobs. We got to an individual practitioner or a small firm: one to five partners, two or three associates each.
- **2**: The job · What job does it do? · A user study turned up the real pains: payment collection, tracking cases (hearing dates get missed) and juggling five or six cases at once. Very little of it is about a CRM.
- **3**: The bench · Which user first? · In B2B you sell to a decision bench: the end user, the decision maker and the finance person. Each has a different pain. Which one will you prioritise?
- **4**: The money · Do people already pay for it? · If payment collection is the pain, why build a CRM? That's a different product line. I was at Razorpay, where B2B payment collections was a core focus, and we couldn't crack it. It sounds easy. It's not.
- **✓**: Narrowed · If you sell to the partners · Then the only thing you can sell is a WhatsApp CRM: an assistant that sits on the WhatsApp groups they're part of. Its only job is to keep context.

*Figure: Four questions turn “a CRM for legal counsels” into one narrow product with one job.*

> **[Advanced]**
>
> ### Advanced Big hire, little hire
>
> There are two moments of hiring. The **big hire** is when someone chooses your product. The **little hire** is each time they actually use it.
>
> Most products are built for the big hire: the landing page, the download. Most die at the little hire: opened twice, never again. A job sentence written for the big hire sounds like a slogan. One written for the little hire sounds like a Tuesday.
>
> That's why PhotoCal's sentence starts at “when my lunch arrives”, not “when I decide to lose weight”. Deciding happens once. Lunch happens every day, and that's where the product is hired or fired. Your first version has to win the little hire.

## Write your job

Write the sentence yourself. You can brainstorm with AI, as long as you do the thinking: when you have the core seed, AI can amplify it, but it can't amplify something that's bad.

**Say this to your agent**

```
Read IDEA_SCOPE.md. Here is my job sentence: [paste it].
Don't rewrite it for me. Check it three ways and tell me which fail:
1. Take my product out of the sentence. Does it still make sense?
2. Does it name a moment I could watch happen?
3. Is there exactly one "so I can", in their words?
Then ask me one question at a time until all three pass.
```

```
## 1. The job
When [the moment it hurts], I want to [what they want right then], so I can [the one thing it lets them do].

(More than one person in the product? Write a job for each, and mark the one you build for this weekend.)

(B2B only) The bench: end user [ ], decision maker [ ], who pays [ ]
```
