# The onboarding journey: first visit to first value

Onboarding isn't a sign-up form. It's the path from opening your link to the moment your product first does its job, asking as little as you can on the way. This chapter gets you that path.

> **By the end of this chapter you'll have**
>
> **The onboarding journey.** The smallest commitment you ask for, the steps from opening your link to the first value, and the worry each step removes.
>
> Example Open the link → one line and one button → the first photo → the first number. **He gives:** one photo of his lunch. No install, no sign-up. **The worry it removes:** “it won't know Indian food”.

## A coffee, not marriage

You can't ask someone to marry you on the first date. You ask them for a coffee.

Onboarding works the same way, for any product, consumer or business. Ask for the lowest commitment you can, and give the most value you can for it, in terms of the job. Once they get a taste, it expands.

Two words. The **first value** is the moment your product first does its job for this person: for Rohan, his own lunch, named, with a number. The **onboarding journey** is every step from opening your link to that moment.

A business example. Say someone is building a coding tool that's 99% cheaper, for engineering teams. Asking a team to move off Claude Code and Codex on day one is a huge commitment. The value is cost, so the onboarding has to show that saving fast, with the smallest commitment: say, one real task on the new tool, with its cost shown.

## Commitment, not the number of steps

Fewer steps doesn't mean fewer people drop off. Show someone four fields (first name, last name, email, phone) or one field asking for their PAN number. More people leave at the PAN number. Think about commitment, not the number of steps.

The biggest commitment most products ask for first is an account. PhotoCal v1 has no login at all: his meals are tied to his phone (how is in How a product works (handbook page #/tech-parts)). If a later version needs one, it asks after the first saved meal: “keep your log?”. How login works is in Connecting the AI (handbook page #/tech-ai); the product decision is where it sits.

Ask first
1. Opens the link
2. Signs up
3. Confirms the email
4. Fills in a profile
5. Watches a tour
6. Taps “Snap your meal”
7. Snaps lunch
8. Sees the number · First value

Give first
1. Opens the link
2. Taps “Snap your meal”
3. Snaps lunch
4. Sees the number · First value
5. Later: keep your log?

*Figure: Same product, same person. Asking first puts the first value at step eight. Giving first puts it at step four, and what's asked after that is asked of someone who has seen it work.*

> **Remember this**
>
> Ask for a coffee first. A question before the first value is a reason to leave; after it, the same question is easy to say yes to.

## Remove the worry about the outcome

> **The rule**
>
> Think about the user first. Think about the job to be done. Then think about the problems, concerns and issues with that outcome: not your product, not you. Solve for those in the onboarding.

People arrive worried about their life, not your buttons: will I actually lose weight? Will I fail again? In chapter 2 you picked the one worry. This is where it goes.

For Rohan it's “it won't know Indian food”. So the first thing PhotoCal asks him to do is photograph his real lunch, and the first thing it shows him is that lunch, named.

- **1**: Opens the link · From a friend's WhatsApp message · Removes: “another app to install?” It opens in the browser.
- **2**: Sees one line and one button · What it does, and “Snap your meal” · Removes: “what do I do?”
- **3**: Takes the first photo · His real lunch, not a sample · Removes: “will it work on my food?”
- **4**: Sees the first number · “Rajma chawal, about 480 kcal” · First value. Removes: “it won't know Indian food.”
- **5**: Fixes it or saves it · One tap either way · Removes: “what if it's wrong?”

*Figure: PhotoCal's onboarding is its core flow done once, with almost nothing in front of it. The big worry from chapter 2 is answered at step 4; each other step removes a smaller one.*

So this weekend you build two things: the core, and one light onboarding that gets someone into it quickly.

**▸ Three kinds of worry, three fixes — Trust, fear and words**

- **Trust**: Some products have a lot of trust issues, like an ad tool that wants your Google Ads account. How others earn trust is something you copy and learn first, then understand why they do it. Search the user flow on Mobbin, a library of real app screens. Don't blindly copy.
- **Fear**: Say you're building a habits app. Everyone has tried building habits since childhood and failed, so confidence is low. Telling them other users did it gives them more FOMO, not confidence. Confidence comes from practice: let them do something very small in the onboarding itself.
- **Words**: Legal people understand the words “connect” and “authenticate”, but the moment they see them they think, I'll ask the younger associate to do it. Explain the step while they do it, and they do it themselves.

## Start small; the scope grows

Will one small step be valuable enough? The job of onboarding is to show you can keep the promise, even in a small way. After that, they'll give you more. If your job takes days to get done, the first value is that first small proof.

It's like a new job. They give you one project first. You do it well, and your scope grows. Just make sure the small thing you ask of them matters to them.

And don't add a screen you weren't going to add. We tend to research more and keep adding, instead of trimming. Don't add.

**▸ Common mistakes — Four ways onboarding gets in the way**

- **Sign-up first**: An account before any value. Ask after the first win.
- **The tour**: Five screens explaining buttons. Let them press one instead.
- **The long profile**: Weight, height, goal and diet before the first photo. Ask each when it pays off.
- **Proof instead of practice**: “10,000 people lost weight with us” creates FOMO, not confidence.

> **[Advanced]**
>
> ### Advanced Setup, aha and habit: three moments, not one
>
> The start of a product has three moments. **Setup**: what they must do to reach the value (open the link, let the browser use the camera). **Aha**: when they first get the value (his own lunch, named, with a number). **Habit**: when they come back for it without being told (he snaps dinner, or tomorrow's lunch).
>
> The usual mistake is to count setup as success: lots of people opened the link, so onboarding works. It doesn't until they reach the aha, and the product doesn't work until some of them reach the habit.
>
> Make each moment countable. Setup: allowed the camera. Aha: saw a first result. Habit: saved meals on three different days in the first week. Those numbers are a choice, not a law; a job that comes up once a month can't have a daily habit.

> **[Advanced]**
>
> ### Advanced Count where people drop off
>
> Once real people open your link, count how many reach each step. The numbers below are made up, to show how to read it.
>
> - **Opened the link**: 20
> - **Tapped “Snap your meal”**: 15
> - **Took a photo**: 12
> - **Saw a result: first value**: 11
> - **Saved it**: 4
> - **Reached the first value**: 11 of 20
>
> *Figure: The biggest drop is where to work next. Here it's saving, not the AI.*
>
> Read the drops between steps, not one final number. Losing five between “opened” and “tapped” is about the first screen. Losing seven between “saw a result” and “saved” says the result didn't feel worth keeping, or saving was hard to find. Then ask two people who dropped there.
>
> **Say this to your agent**
>
> ```
> Read PRODUCT.md, section 4 (the onboarding journey).
> Add the smallest possible step log: each time someone reaches one of those steps, save the step name and the time in a Convex table. No names, no emails, no photos.
> Then give me one simple way to see how many people reached each step today. Tell me your plan before you write code.
> ```

## Write your onboarding journey

Start from the worry you chose in chapter 2 and the first value of your core flow. Write the smallest commitment that gets from one to the other, and the worry each step removes. If two people are in your product, write it for the one you build for first.

**PRODUCT.md · 4. Onboarding**

```
## 4. Onboarding
First value (the moment it first does the job for them): [what they see]
The smallest commitment we ask for: [what they give, and nothing more]
The worry it removes (from section 2): [their worry about the outcome, in their words]

From opening the link to the first value:
1. [where they come from, and what they open] removes: [worry]
2. [ ] removes: [ ]
3. [the first value]

Login: [not in v1 / after step [n], because ...]
What we don't ask on day one: [profile, tour, permissions, ...]
What we ask later, and when: [question] after [moment]
```
