# PART 1. THE INTENT SPEC

# What a handbook must deliver (intent spec)

Draft, 9 Oct 2026 (Tanisha, with Claude). The standard a generated handbook is judged against: by the delivery reviewer, by evals, and later by the prompts themselves. It names no topics on purpose: every rule must hold for anything a person can type. PRODUCT.md says why the product exists; this page says what a reader must get. Edit freely; the reviewer reads whatever is here.

## 1. The promise

A person types the thing they keep meaning to learn, picks a goal, and gets a handbook made for that goal: a plan, then chapters of swipeable cards on a phone. Each sitting leaves them with something they can show: they can now do it, explain it, use it, or follow it. They feel they are moving, not collecting.

## 2. The shape comes from the reader's goal, never from the topic alone

Four questions decide every handbook. They are answered for this reader, from the goal and the level. The same topic can need a very different handbook for a different goal.

1. **What does the reader walk away with?** An ability (they can do it), an understanding (they can explain it), a result (something made, fixed or decided), a story they can follow, or a resource they keep and come back to.
2. **Breadth:** which separate pieces does that need? A piece is one thing the reader can use on its own.
3. **Depth:** how much does each piece need, for this reader? Light (a few minutes), medium (a proper sitting), deep (a long sitting, or more than one).
4. **Pace:** must the reader practise, act in the real world, or let ideas settle between sittings?

What follows from them:
- **3 to 7 chapters.** Even a small request gets 3 chapters, each direct and useful on its own. Never more than 7.
- **A chapter is one sitting** that holds whole pieces: several light ones, one or two medium ones, or one deep one (a deep piece can take two chapters). Never half a piece, never padding, never a chapter of background.
- **Chapter length follows depth:** a light chapter is short, a deep chapter is long. Length is never fixed in advance.
- **Pace decides quick or course:** a course is read one chapter a day; a quick handbook is read in one or two sittings. The number of chapters does not decide it.
- Every combination is valid: one piece or many, light or deep, quick or course. No shape is the default.

## 3. Every chapter

- **Delivers what it promises, complete and ready to use:** every step, amount, command, check, option or event the reader needs for its pieces. The reader should not need another source to act on it.
- **Goes straight to it.** The hook and the voice wrap around the content; they never replace it.
- **Starts where this reader's knowledge stops.** A foundation the reader lacks is taught through a small real use of it; a prep step is never its own chapter.
- **Reads well on a phone:** one idea per card, short cards, plain words, a new term explained where it first appears, nothing tested before it is taught.
- **Holds attention honestly:** a hook that is specific and true, a question kept open inside the chapter and paid off, a narrator with opinions, examples as small scenes, a light moment where it fits (warmth instead on money, health, legal, loss and emotional topics). These must not turn into tics: the same opener, the same closing formula, fear-framing, forced jokes, stacked metaphors.
- **Ends honestly:** a line that leads to the next chapter when there is one; on the last chapter, what the reader can now do.
- **Uses an analogy only where it truly helps.** A course that teaches a skill, a system or an idea usually has one good picture, used in the chapters where it fits. A forced picture is worse than none.
- Chapter 1 is the way in: short, no quizzes, the central thing seen or done early.

## 4. Never

- **Never wrong.** No invented facts, names, dates, numbers, quotes or causes. A number on an invented person is an example, not a measurement. Absolute words ("always", "never", "exactly") must be true. Events after today are described as announced or expected, never as what happens. Figures of speech are fine when no reader would take them literally.
- **Never unsafe.** A step that can hurt carries its one precaution at that step. Physical skills with risk state the safety condition first and teach how to stop or get out safely before the move. Money, health and legal topics show the downside of a risk and say who to ask (a SEBI-registered adviser, a doctor, a lawyer). Never personal advice, never a personal choice called a mistake.
- **Never foreign to the reader.** Unless the reader says otherwise they live in India: Indian names, rupees, metric units, Indian places, institutions and rules first.
- **Never harmful.** The product does not teach harm; it offers the good version or declines kindly.

## 5. What a gap is

Anything in a delivered handbook that breaks sections 2 to 4, or that a reader would notice: a shape that does not fit the goal (too short, too long, wrong pace), a chapter that does not deliver its pieces, a starting point that loses or bores the reader, a card where a reader would stop, an error, a safety miss, a foreign default. Every gap is traced to the step that caused it: research (the shape and the facts), plan (the chapters and their pieces), writer (the chapter), fact check (what it let through or broke), or code (what it overrode, dropped or never passed on).

### Chapter 1 of 4: Pick and sift your sponge: the right lentils
[card 1] picture
Gluey dal happens when loose surface starch turns to paste in your pot instead of washing down the drain.

Think of dal as a **three-layer cake**. Your boiled lentils are the soft sponge that holds everything up. Rinsing them is like sifting flour: you wash off dusty starch so the texture stays light and creamy.

[card 2] teach: Pick your grain
For classic yellow dal, look for *toor dal*, which means split pigeon peas. They taste nutty and break down into a smooth, creamy base.

If you want dinner fast, pick *masoor dal*, which are split red lentils. They cook faster than unsoaked toor dal on a standard stovetop. Both yield that familiar bright yellow bowl.

[card 3] example: At the tap
Priya scoops 1 cup of toor dal into a glass bowl and runs cold tap water over it. The water turns milky gray in two seconds.

She swirls the lentils with her fingers, tips the cloudy water out, and refills the bowl. After four rinses, the water stays clear. She covers them in clean water to soak.

[card 4] mistake
Arun dumped dry lentils straight into boiling water without rinsing or soaking them first.

The pot foamed over with sticky white scum within minutes, and the grains stayed tough in the center after forty minutes. Rinsing removes excess starch; **soaking 20 to 30 minutes** cuts your cooking time significantly.

[card 5] try
Measure 1 cup of toor or masoor dal into a bowl. Rinse 3 to 4 times in cold water until clear, then leave submerged in water to soak while you read.

[card 6] teach: In one breath
Wash your lentils three to four times to strip off sticky starch, then soak them for twenty minutes so they cook evenly.

### Chapter 2 of 4: Bake the sponge: simmering lentils soft
[card 1] picture
Half a teaspoon of one spice turns a pot of beige lentils bright golden yellow.

In our layer cake, these simmered lentils are **the plain sponge cake**: soft, mild, and built to absorb every bold flavor you drop on top later. If your sponge comes out hard or watery, the whole dessert collapses.

[card 2] teach: Water, heat, and gold
To cook tender lentils, the math is simple: use **one cup of dry lentils to three or four cups of fresh water**.

Add half a teaspoon of ground *turmeric*, the dried golden root powder that gives Indian lentil curry its sunny color. Bring the water to a boil, then dial the flame down so it sits at a gentle simmer.

Too little liquid leaves your pot scorched before the grains soften. Too much leaves you with thin lentil tea instead of a rich, creamy base.

[card 3] example: A pot on the stove
Rohan dumps his cup of soaked, drained toor dal into a heavy pot. He pours in three and a half cups of water and whisks in half a teaspoon of turmeric.

As the water reaches a rolling simmer, the beige water shifts into a glowing marigold yellow. He lowers the burner to medium-low and lets the pot bubble uncovered. Within fifteen minutes, the kitchen smells earthy and warm, and the hard edges of the split lentils begin to blur into the broth.

[card 4] teach: The pressure test
You have two ways to finish cooking. A stovetop pressure cooker takes **three to four whistles**, the rhythmic steam blasts that sound as pressure builds inside, before you kill the heat.

In an open pot on your stove, expect **twenty-five to thirty-five minutes** of bubbling.

You know it is ready by feel, not just the clock. Press a swollen lentil against the inside wall of the pan with the back of a wooden spoon. It should give way completely into a smooth mush, with no chalky white pebble hiding in the center.

[card 5] try
Spoon one hot lentil onto a plate and mash it with your thumb. If it flattens like soft butter, pull the pot off the heat.

[card 6] mistake
A common trap is pulling the pan off the burner the second the lentils look bloated. 

Neha saw soft grains floating in yellow broth after twenty minutes and killed the fire, assuming hot liquid would finish softening them on the counter. Dinner ended with **gritty, sandy bites** in every mouthful. Lentils do not carry over and soften off the heat; they must crush effortlessly in the pan before you stop.

[card 7] teach: In one breath
Simmer one cup of lentils in three to four cups of water with half a teaspoon of turmeric until they crush flat under a spoon without any grit. That creates the tender, golden sponge base that will hold tonight's dinner together.

Next: why garlic and ginger need exactly sixty seconds in the pan.

Outcome line: You can now cook lentils until they mash easily against the side of the pot.

### Chapter 3 of 4: Make the filling: onion, ginger, garlic, tomato
[card 1] picture
How long should minced garlic and ginger hit the pan? **About sixty seconds**, because much longer and the garlic starts to burn and turn bitter.

In our layer-cake dal, your soft simmered lentils are the plain sponge. This spiced onion and tomato mixture is the rich, jammy filling spread straight through the middle. Without it, your pot is just wet boiled grain; with it, every spoonful carries deep savory warmth.

[card 2] teach: Sweet onions and sixty seconds
Set a heavy saucepan over medium heat and melt two tablespoons of *ghee*—butter with its milk solids strained out so it never scorches—or neutral oil. Tip in one finely chopped yellow onion.

Take your time here. Let the onions cook until their edges turn a deep golden brown, roughly eight to ten minutes. When you rush onions, you get crunchy raw bits instead of mellow sweetness.

Once that sweetness is locked in, stir in your minced fresh ginger and garlic. Give them **exactly sixty seconds** in the hot fat. As soon as a warm perfume rises from the pan, move immediately to the next step.

[card 3] teach: Cooking down to jam
Moisture stops garlic from burning, so drop in two finely chopped ripe tomatoes right at the sixty-second mark. Sprinkle in half a teaspoon of ground coriander and a quarter teaspoon of red chili powder.

Stir everything together over medium heat. As the tomatoes cook, their water evaporates and their acid softens, collapsing them into a **thick, jammy paste** over five to seven minutes. Look for tiny beads of oil separating along the skillet's edges.

Once that oil glistens, fold your warm cooked lentils straight into the pan. Stir gently to let the plain sponge drink up the savory base.

[card 4] example: Priya builds the base
Priya stood over her skillet on a Tuesday night with her cooked pot of yellow lentils resting on the back burner. She warmed two tablespoons of ghee, tipped in a diced onion, and waited patiently until the edges browned like toasted hazelnuts.

She tossed in two minced garlic cloves and a knob of grated ginger. Sixty seconds later, her kitchen filled with a sweet, nutty aroma. Before the garlic could take on color, she dumped in two chopped vine tomatoes, coriander, and chili.

Five minutes later, the tomatoes collapsed into glossy crimson jam. She poured in her warm lentils and stirred; the pale yellow turned a rich sunset gold.

[card 5] mistake
The costliest mistake is tossing garlic into the pan at the very start alongside raw onions. Marcus did this to save a step, but onions need eight to ten minutes to brown, while minced garlic burns in a couple of minutes.

By the time his onions browned, charred black specks covered the skillet, leaving the entire pot **tasting acrid and bitter**. If your garlic ever smells like burnt toast instead of sweet roasted cloves, wipe the pan clean and start over.

[card 6] try
Brown your chopped onion in two tablespoons of warm ghee or oil, stir in minced garlic and ginger for sixty seconds, then cook two tomatoes until jammy before folding in your simmered lentils.

[card 7] teach: In one breath
Brown your onions slowly until sweet, give garlic and ginger just sixty seconds in the fat, and cook your tomatoes into a rich jam before stirring in the lentils. That savory base turns plain boiled pulses into a deeply comforting dish.

Next: cumin and mustard seeds pop in ten seconds in hot ghee for the crackling tadka finish.

Outcome line: You can now build a savory onion-tomato base and fold the cooked lentils into it.

### Chapter 4 of 4: Pour the icing: tadka and the finish
[card 1] picture
Cumin and mustard seeds pop within ten seconds in hot ghee, and that tiny sizzle is your entire cooking window.

Think of this step as **pouring the icing** onto your cake. The simmered lentils were the soft sponge, the onion-tomato masala was the filling, and now comes the hot, fragrant fat that carries all the drama straight to the table.

[card 2] teach: The ten-second sizzle
In Indian kitchens, this step is called *tadka*, which simply means blooming whole spices in hot fat to pull out their rich aromatic oils.

Warm two tablespoons of *ghee*, which is clarified butter with the milk solids strained out so it tolerates high heat without scorching. Set your small pan over medium heat until a single cumin seed bubbles immediately when dropped in.

Now add half a teaspoon of cumin seeds and half a teaspoon of mustard seeds. They will crackle, swell, and jump in **less than ten seconds**.

[card 3] example: A crackle in real time
Priya lines up her ingredients on a small plate right beside the burner before turning on the flame: cumin, brown mustard seeds, two whole dried red chilies, and a tiny pinch of *hing*, also called asafoetida, an aromatic plant resin that gives dal its signature garlicky, savory hum.

Once her ghee melts and shimmers, she slides in the cumin and mustard seeds. They immediately pop like miniature firecrackers. Within five seconds she tosses in the chilies and hing, gives the pan one gentle swirl, and **pulls it off the heat** before anything can brown too far.

[card 4] try
Measure your cumin, mustard seeds, chilies, and hing onto a single saucer before lighting the stove so you never scramble mid-sizzle.

[card 5] mistake
The single most common slip is looking away to chop a garnish while whole spices sit in hot ghee.

Whole seeds transition from toasted and nutty to **black and acrid** in roughly four seconds. If the seeds turn dark brown or smoke heavily, do not pour them into your pot. Tip that batch into the trash, wipe the pan, and start fresh with two clean tablespoons of ghee.

[card 6] teach: The pour and the sprinkles
The second the spices finish blooming, carry the pan straight to your pot and pour the sizzling mixture directly over the lentils. It will roar with a loud crackle.

Clamp a lid onto the pot immediately for two minutes. This **traps the fragrant steam**, forcing all those toasted spice oils down into the lentil base.

Uncover the pot and finish with your sprinkles: stir in one teaspoon of fresh lemon juice for brightness, then scatter a handful of chopped fresh coriander leaves across the top.

[card 7] try
Stir one teaspoon of fresh lemon juice through your pot just before serving to snap the rich ghee into balance.

[card 8] teach: In one breath
Tadka is quick: bloom your seeds in hot ghee for ten seconds, drop in the chilies and hing, and pour the crackling fat straight over your cooked lentils.

Trap the steam under a lid, stir in a splash of lemon juice and fresh coriander, and your bowl is complete.

Next: steaming fluffy jeera rice to catch every savory drop of your yellow dal.

Outcome line: You can now make a sizzling spice tadka without burning the seeds and finish your dal with bright lemon and coriander.

### Chapter 1 of 3: The Starting Drape
[card 1] doit: Do it now (checklist)
Set your shirt and drape your tie before a mirror.
  - Button the top collar button
  - Flip the entire collar up
  - Rest seams flat against your shirt
  - Set the wide end 12 inches below the narrow end (target 1)

[card 2] teach: The Twelve-Inch Rule
Most ties finish too short or awkwardly crooked because of **one missed measurement** before the first turn.

Button your dress shirt all the way to the top button, then flip the collar up completely. Drape the tie around your neck with the seam facing your chest. The wide end must hang about twelve inches lower than the narrow end.

[card 3] move: Set the Drape
Cues: Flip the entire collar up | Face the seams toward your shirt | Lower the wide blade twelve inches

[card 4] mistake: The Even Ends
Marcus draped both ends of his tie at equal length before knotting. Because the knot uses up fabric from the wide end (the broad, wrapping end), his finished tie stopped awkwardly across his chest.

Never start with both ends level. That **twelve-inch head start** gives the wide end enough fabric to wrap, knot, and still reach your waist.

[card 5] teach: In one breath
Fasten your collar button, flip the collar up, and give the wide end a twelve-inch lead over the narrow tail. Getting this foundation right helps your knot finish at the right height.

Next: crossing the wide end and locking your first secure wrap.

Outcome line: You can position your shirt collar and starting tie lengths so your knot finishes at the right height.

### Chapter 2 of 3: Wrapping the Four-in-Hand
[card 1] teach: The wrap and the window
The secret to the Four-in-Hand is that the narrow end never moves. It stays completely still like a center pole, while the wide end does all the work.

You will cross the wide end over the front, take it behind the narrow tail, bring it across the front again to form a front loop (a band of fabric across your chest), and then pull it up through the neck loop (the part around your throat) from underneath.

Keep your thumb lightly planted on that front loop. That loop is the opening your tie will slide down through in the very next step.

[card 2] move: Cross and wrap
Cues: Cross wide over narrow near your collarbone | Pass wide behind the narrow tail horizontally | Bring wide across the front to create a band

[card 3] doit: Do it now (reps)
Stand at your mirror and wrap the wide end around the narrow tail twice to form the horizontal front band. (target 3)

[card 4] move: Up and through
Cues: Push wide end up behind the neck loop from underneath | Pull wide end over the top toward your chin | Slide wide end downward through the front band

[card 5] doit: Do it now (reps)
Complete the full wrap from cross to threading down through the front loop. (target 3)

[card 6] mistake: Letting the narrow tail twist
People often pull the wide end (the thick part) so hard that the narrow tail spins around and bunches up. If that happens, the knot locks in place and will not slide smoothly up toward your collar.

Keep a loose pinch on the narrow tail with your non-dominant hand (the one you don't write with) while wrapping. The narrow tail should stay flat against your shirt buttons the entire time.

[card 7] teach: In one breath
Keep the narrow end dead still against your chest, wrap the wide end around it into a front loop, then pull the wide end up through the neck loop and down through the front loop.

Next: pulling the knot snug, landing the tip on the belt buckle, and pinching the dimple (a small fold under the knot).

Outcome line: You can now wrap and thread the Four-in-Hand knot smoothly without losing tail tension.

### Chapter 3 of 3: Snugging, Length, and the Dimple
[card 1] doit: Do it now (checklist)
Set the dimple, snug the knot to your collar, and check the length.
  - Hold the narrow tail and slide the knot upward toward your top button.
  - Press your index finger into the middle of the blade right beneath the knot to crease a dimple.
  - Check that the pointed tip of the wide end touches the center of your belt buckle.
  - Slide the loose narrow tail through the keeper loop on the back of the wide blade.
  - Fold down your shirt collar all around so the knot sits snug between collar points. (target 5)

[card 2] teach: The Final Polish
A single fingertip pinch under the knot turns an everyday tie into a crisp, professional look.

To finish, hold the narrow tail firmly with one hand and slide the knot upward toward your top shirt button with the other. As you slide, press your index finger right into the middle of the wide blade just beneath the knot. That pocket forms a *dimple*—a centered fold in the fabric that catches light and gives the knot structure.

Once snug, check your waist: the pointed tip of the wide end must touch the center of your **belt buckle**. Tuck the loose tail into the *keeper loop*, the fabric strap on the back of the wide blade, and fold your collar down all around.

[card 3] move: Cinch and Dimple
Cues: Grip narrow tail firmly | Press index into center | Slide knot against button

[card 4] mistake
The most common mistake is yanking on a finished knot to fix a bad length. Tugging stretches the silk out of shape and twists the knot sideways. If the pointed tip sits inches above or below your belt buckle, untie it completely and shift your starting drape instead.

[card 5] teach: In one breath
Slide the knot up against your fastened collar button while pinching a center dimple, tuck the tail into the keeper loop, and rest the tip right on your belt buckle.

Next: ironing your dress shirt so your sharp tie sits against a crisp collar.

Outcome line: You can now cinch your Four-in-Hand knot, center the tip on your belt buckle, and pinch an interview-ready dimple.

### Chapter 1 of 4: Measure the flow at the tap
[card 1] picture
Before you touch a single setting: is it even your Wi-Fi that is slow?

Think of your internet like the home water supply: the city pipe brings water to the house, and your router is the sprinkler that sprays it into each room. If the main pipe has barely any flow, adjusting the sprinkler will not help. You must measure the water at the tap first.

[card 2] steps: Check the tap
  1. Stand right beside your router with your phone or laptop. → Full Wi-Fi signal indicator icons on your device screen.
  2. Open a web browser, navigate to Speedtest.net, and select Go. → A dial moves and displays your download speed in Mbps (megabits per second, the speed data arrives).
  3. Compare that download number against the speed tier listed on your internet bill. → Whether your incoming stream matches what you pay for.

[card 3] mistake
Elena spent two hours rebooting her router because calls dropped in the back bedroom. When she ran Speedtest.net right next to the router, she got full speed.

Her provider was delivering every megabit (a small unit of data); the wireless spray was just losing power across three walls. **Never test from the couch**, or you will blame the wrong piece of gear.

[card 4] try
Plug a computer directly into your modem with an *Ethernet cable*—a physical network wire with plastic clips. Run Speedtest.net again. If a direct wire is fast but nearby Wi-Fi is slow, your router is the real bottleneck.

[card 5] teach: In one breath
If your speed test beside the router matches your bill, your incoming service is fine and distance or walls are choking the Wi-Fi. If a wired cable test is slow, the street pipe is choked and only your provider can fix it.

### Chapter 1 of 4: Snow that sticks
[card 1] picture
Soon you'll see how $100 can earn an extra 25 cents in year two. That comes from **interest earning interest**.

Your money behaves like a snowball rolling down a long hill. With every turn it gathers new snow, and as long as that snow sticks, the enlarged ball grabs even more snow on the very next roll.

[card 2] teach: Brushing off the snow
Imagine you deposit $100 into an account paying 5% interest per year. That starting deposit is your *principal*, which simply means the money you put in yourself.

With *simple interest*, interest paid only on your starting money, you collect $5 each year. If you sweep that $5 away, year two earns the exact same flat $5. Your snowball never grows.

[card 3] example: The extra twenty-five cents
Now let that snow stick. Elena deposits $100 at 5% annual interest. After twelve months, she earns $5, lifting her balance to $105.

Instead of withdrawing the $5, she leaves it alone. In year two, 5% applies to the entire $105 balance. That pays her $5.25, leaving her with $110.25. That spare quarter was born purely because her first $5 went to work.

[card 4] teach: Snow that sticks
Leaving those earnings in place creates *compound interest*, which means earning interest on your initial deposit and on accumulated interest together.

Under simple interest, Elena would have $110 after two years. Compound interest gave her $110.25. Twenty-five cents seems tiny today, but every extra cent enlarges the rolling snowball. Over decades, that growing snowball turns a gentle slope into an avalanche.

[card 5] mistake: Cashing out the gains
The common mistake is pocketing the new interest as fast as it arrives.

Mark earned $5 on his first $100 and withdrew it to buy lunch. By peeling off the fresh layer of snow every year, he kept his snowball at its starting size. Compounding only works its magic when the new snow stays glued to the ball.

[card 6] teach: In one breath

### Chapter 2 of 4: How often the ball turns
[card 1] picture
Two accounts offer the exact same 5% interest rate, yet one quietly leaves you with more cash at year's end.

Think back to our snowball rolling downhill: the hill's length is time, but **compounding frequency is how often the ball turns over**. Every full turn picks up fresh snow and packs it onto the surface right away.

[card 2] teach: Decoding the snowball formula
Banks track this rolling ball with one famous formula: *A = P(1 + r/n)^(nt)*. It looks intimidating, but every letter is just a part of the snow hill.

**P is principal**, the initial deposit you place on the hill. The letter *r* is the annual interest rate, written as a decimal like 0.05 for 5%. The letter *t* is time, measuring how many years the ball rolls.

Then comes *n*, which stands for **compounding frequency**, or how many times per year the bank credits earned interest back into your balance. When an account compounds monthly, *n* equals 12. If it compounds daily, *n* is 365.

[card 3] try
Check your own bank or savings app tonight. Search for "interest schedule" or "APY terms" to see whether your cash compounds monthly, daily, or only once a year.

[card 4] example: Elena's twin bank accounts
Elena splits $10,000 evenly between two accounts to see the difference for herself. Both accounts advertise an identical 6% annual rate.

Account A compounds annually, meaning *n* is 1. The snowball turns over once a year, paying Elena exactly $300 at the end of twelve months on her $5,000.

Account B compounds monthly, so *n* is 12. Instead of waiting all year, the bank chops the rate into monthly slices of 0.5% and adds that fresh snow every month. By month two, Elena earns interest on month one's payout. Over the year, Account B yields $308.39.

**More frequent compounding credits interest sooner**, giving your earnings extra months to earn their own interest.

[card 5] mistake
Marcus shopped for high-yield savings and assumed every account with a 5% headline rate pays the exact same profit.

He picked an account crediting interest just once a year over one crediting interest daily. Because the daily account turns the snowball 365 times rather than once, it adds fresh snow every single night. Marcus lost free money simply by ignoring **how often the interest gets locked in**.

### Chapter 1 of 5: Why Git stops, and how to tear up the card
[card 1] picture
Git never guesses which change should win; it **stops and asks you**, and that pause is the safest thing it can do.

Think of it like you and a friend photocopying the same recipe card. If you both rewrite the exact same line differently, nobody can guess which seasoning was intended. Git holds both copies out and waits.

[card 2] steps: Trigger and abort a conflict
  1. mkdir scratch && cd scratch && git init && echo 'sugar' > recipe.txt && git add recipe.txt && git commit -m 'base' → Created root commit with recipe.txt containing 'sugar'.
  2. git checkout -b friend && echo 'honey' > recipe.txt && git commit -am 'friend edit' → Committed update on branch friend.
  3. git checkout - && echo 'maple' > recipe.txt && git commit -am 'my edit' && git merge friend → CONFLICT (content): Merge conflict in recipe.txt. Automatic merge failed.
  4. git merge --abort → Clean working tree restored; recipe.txt is back to 'maple'.

[card 3] teach: The three-way compare
Git does not just look at your file and your friend's file. It finds the *common ancestor*—the original recipe card you both copied before branching.

By comparing all three copies, Git sees who changed what. If only one person touched a line, Git takes it automatically. It halts only when you **both altered the same line differently**.

[card 4] mistake
When a conflict stops everything, many people panic and delete files by hand or force-kill their shell.

Running `git status` shows your disputed file waiting under **Unmerged paths**. If you ever feel lost or want a clean slate, running `git merge --abort` instantly returns your repository to the state right before you merged.

[card 5] teach: In one breath
Git pauses merges because guessing would quietly destroy work; it compares both branches against their shared ancestor and asks for help only on overlapping edits. Whenever you want to back out, **git merge --abort** cleanly resets everything.

### Chapter 2 of 5: Reading both versions on the card
[card 1] picture
Those angle brackets in your file are a map, and you can learn to read it in minutes. When Git halts a merge, it does not destroy your code; it pastes both copies directly onto the same page.

Think of the recipe card where you and your friend wrote over the exact same line: Git draws a **heavy divider line** between your notes and theirs so you can read both scribbles side by side.

[card 2] steps: Inspect the conflict markers
  1. Run git status in your terminal → The conflicted file listed in red under Unmerged paths
  2. Open the conflicted file in your text editor → Seven angle brackets highlighting the disputed lines
  3. Spot <<<<<<< HEAD at the start of the block → The exact lines written on your active branch
  4. Look past ======= to find >>>>>>> branch-name → The incoming branch changes sitting right below the divider

[card 3] teach: The three parts of the fence
Git writes exactly **three marker lines** into your file to frame every disputed section. The top marker begins with seven less-than signs followed by *HEAD*, which marks the branch you were standing on when you ran the merge command.

The middle marker is a clean row of seven equal signs. It acts as a strict divider, separating your changes from the incoming text without adding any code of its own.

The bottom marker uses seven greater-than signs tagged with the incoming branch name. Everything between the equal signs and that final tag belongs to the branch you are pulling into your work.

[card 4] example: Elena reads the pasta sauce
Elena merged the branch *garlic-upgrade* into *main* and opened `sauce.py`. Right in the middle of her sauce simmering function, Git left this exact block:

<<<<<<< HEAD
simmer(heat='low', minutes=25)
=======
simmer(heat='medium', minutes=15)
>>>>>>> garlic-upgrade

Elena did not guess. Because *HEAD* was pinned to the top marker, she instantly recognized the 25-minute simmer at low heat as **her current branch version**. The 15-minute simmer at medium heat below the equal signs came straight from *garlic-upgrade*. She saw both versions clearly before touching a single character.

[card 5] exercise (apply)
You run git merge api-v2 while working on main, and you see this block in config.js:

<<<<<<< HEAD
const TIMEOUT = 5000;
=======
const TIMEOUT = 1500;
>>>>>>> api-v2

Which value belongs to the incoming api-v2 branch?
  a) The 5000 ms value sitting between the HEAD marker and the equal signs.
  b) Both values combined together because Git merged the branches halfway.
  c) The 1500 ms value sitting between the equal signs and the api-v2 marker.   ← answer
  reteach: Git always places the code from your currently checked-out branch at the top between HEAD and the equal signs. The incoming branch changes are always placed below the equal signs, right above the closing marker labeled with that branch's name.

[card 6] mistake: Assuming the top half is the base
Marcus opened a conflicted file, looked at the lines under *HEAD*, and assumed they were the untouched original code from two weeks ago. He erased them, believing he was wiping out stale history, and accidentally threw away his own fresh work.

**HEAD is your current work**, never the ancient baseline. When you look at the top half, you are looking at the commits you made on your active branch today, not the shared ancestor commit you both started from.

[card 7] exercise (recall)
While on the feature branch auth-flow, you merge origin/main and find this block in routes.py:

<<<<<<< HEAD
redirect('/dashboard')
=======
redirect('/onboarding')
>>>>>>> origin/main

If you want to keep only your local branch's change, which line do you keep?
  a) The line redirect('/dashboard') because the top marker holds the HEAD edits.   ← answer
  b) Neither line because both markers must be kept to let the merge complete.
  c) The line redirect('/onboarding') because the bottom marker holds local edits.
  reteach: The tag HEAD indicates the branch you are actively working on before the merge began. Any lines between the HEAD marker and the divider line of equal signs are your branch's changes, while the lines below the divider come from the incoming branch.

[card 8] teach: In one breath
The markers in a conflicted file are just brackets: **HEAD holds your branch's lines at the top**, seven equal signs divide the fight, and the incoming branch sits at the bottom.

Once you know which side wrote which line, deciding what survives is straightforward editing.

### Chapter 2 of 5: Frame it, then snap the photo
[card 1] picture: Setting up the viewfinder
Why does Git make you choose what is in the frame before you can snap anything?

Think of your project folder as a photo set you are constantly rearranging. Your *working directory*—the ordinary folder on your laptop where you create and edit files—is full of mess: half-written thoughts, draft notes, and test tweaks. You do not want every scrap cluttering the picture.

Git gives you a viewfinder called the **staging area**, a holding zone where you arrange only the specific files you want in your next shot.

[card 2] teach: The two-step dance
Most tools save your work with a single click, but Git intentionally separates preparing changes from saving them. The routine has two deliberate beats: stage, then snap.

When you run `git add <file>`, you move changes into the staging area. You are telling Git: put this file inside the camera frame. Files you leave untouched on the set stay behind for later.

Once the frame holds exactly what you want, you run `git commit -m "message"`. That snaps a **commit**, a permanent snapshot of the entire project, tagged with a unique ID code (a long string of letters and numbers). If you never staged it, it never enters the photo.

[card 3] try: Check your status
Open your terminal (the window where you type commands) in the folder of your project copy. Type `git status` to see what is currently sitting on your set versus what is staged in the frame.

[card 4] try: Try it
Tap to toggle changed files between your messy set and the framed staging area, then watch `git status` shift colors before you commit.

[card 5] steps: Stage and commit changes
  1. git status → Untracked or modified files appear in red under Changes not staged for commit.
  2. git add README.md → The file README.md moves under Changes to be committed, printed in green.
  3. git commit -m "Update onboarding instructions in README" → A confirmation line appears with [main 4f1a2b3] and 1 file changed, 1 insertion(+).
  4. git status → nothing to commit, working tree clean

[card 6] mistake: Committing the whole set at once
A new teammate edited three lines in `billing.py`, opened a config file to inspect a database port, accidentally typed a space, and ran `git add .` to grab everything.

Their commit message said *Fix checkout calculation*, but their snapshot bundled the accidental spacing tweak alongside it. A teammate reviewing the change wasted ten minutes wondering why database settings were touched.

Before running `git add`, always run `git status`. Stage only the files that belong to the single story you are telling.

[card 8] teach: In one breath

### Chapter 1 of 5: Lay out the city, send out the long-range guards
[card 1] picture
Bishops never change colour, all game long. Here is why: bishops can only slice along *diagonals*—slanted paths of squares touching corner to corner—and a diagonal step always lands on a square of the same colour, so a bishop on a light square is locked to light squares forever.

Every piece is a **bodyguard protecting your VIP king**. Some guards strike close, but three command the long open streets.

[card 2] teach: Paving the city grid
Before any guard moves, set the board. The golden rule is **white on the right**: make sure a light square sits in your bottom-right corner.

White lines up on ranks 1 and 2; Black takes ranks 7 and 8 (ranks are the numbered horizontal rows). Back corners hold rooks, then knights, then bishops. In the centre, the **queen takes her own colour**: the white queen on light d1, the black queen on dark d8.

[card 3] teach: The long-range guards
Now meet your three long-range snipers.

**Rooks move in straight lines** along ranks or *files*—the lettered vertical columns—traveling as far as an open path allows.

**Bishops slice diagonally** across empty squares of their starting shade.

**The queen does both**. She combines rook and bishop into one powerhouse, racing straight or diagonal along any clear path until another piece stands in the way.

[card 4] try

[card 5] mistake
The most common setup blunder is swapping the king and queen. A beginner puts the white king on the light centre square, and the whole army faces the wrong way.

To avoid this, remember that **the queen dresses to match her floor**. The white queen sits on light d1, while the black queen sits on dark d8, right beside her king.

### Chapter 2 of 5: The odd guards: knights, pawns and the VIP
[card 1] picture
Only one piece on the board can leap straight over others, and it is the trickiest bodyguard you command. While your long-range guards sweep clear corridors across the 64 squares of the city grid, the knight vaults cleanly over crowded traffic.

Behind them sits your **king, the VIP who can only step one square in any direction**. If your VIP is attacked and has no escape, the game ends. But to protect him, you must master the quirky footwork of his strangest defenders.

[card 2] teach: The leaping knight and the stepping king
The knight moves in an **L-shape of two squares in one direction and one square sideways**. It is the only chess piece that can jump over other pieces on the board, whether friendly or hostile.

A knight hops over obstacles without disturbing them, landing only on its destination square. If an enemy occupies that final square, the knight captures it by removing that piece from the board.

Your king moves far more modestly. The king steps **exactly one square at a time in any direction**: up, down, sideways, or diagonally. He has immense authority, but the VIP has very short legs.

[card 3] try: Trace the knight hop
Place your finger on a light square. Slide two squares forward, then take one square directly to the side. Notice how your finger lands on a dark square: a knight **always changes square colour** on every single legal jump.

[card 4] example: Marcus clears the roadblock
Marcus sat across from his nephew Leo. Leo had walled off the board, jamming pawns together in the centre. No bishop could slide past, and Marcus could not roll his rook down the file, which is a vertical column of eight squares.

Marcus reached for his knight on the b1 square. He lifted the piece, sailed straight over his own pawns, and dropped it onto the c3 square.

Leo blinked, assuming the crowded barricade blocked all traffic. But the **knight ignores every wall**, landing cleanly on its target square while leaving every piece beneath its arc untouched.

[card 5] exercise (apply)
Your white knight sits on b1, completely boxed in by friendly pawns on a2, b2, and c2. Which move can your knight legally make?
  a) Jump over the pawns to land on the open c3 square.   ← answer
  b) Slide forward one square onto b2 and push the friendly pawn.
  c) Dash diagonally past the pawns onto a3 like a bishop.
  reteach: Think about how this piece differs from the others: it does not need an open path and cannot shove friendly pieces aside. Check which option matches its own special way of travelling.

[card 6] teach: Pawns march forward but strike diagonally
Pawns are the foot soldiers holding the front line. You might expect them to capture whatever blocks their path, but **pawns move straight and capture diagonally**.

A pawn marches forward one square at a time along its file, never backward. However, on its very first move from rank 2 for White or rank 7 for Black, a pawn has the option to surge forward two squares.

Here is the twist: a pawn cannot move forward if another piece blocks its square. It captures an enemy piece only by **stepping one square diagonally forward**, replacing the opposing piece on that diagonal square.

[card 7] mistake: The head-on collision
Leo pushed his pawn two squares forward to e4 on his first turn. Marcus immediately pushed his own pawn forward to e5, placing the two wooden figures nose-to-nose.

On the next turn, Leo tried to grab Marcus's pawn off the board, thinking he could bulldoze straight ahead. Marcus gently stopped Leo's hand.

When two opposing pawns face each other directly, they are completely locked. A **pawn cannot capture straight ahead**, so both foot soldiers stay frozen until another piece intervenes or a diagonal target appears.

[card 8] exercise (apply)
White has a pawn on e4. Black has a knight on e5 directly in front of it, and a black pawn on d5 diagonally to the left. What can the white pawn do?
  a) Jump straight over the knight to e6 with a two-square move.
  b) Step diagonally to d5 and capture the black pawn there.   ← answer
  c) March straight into e5 and capture the black knight.
  reteach: Look at what stands directly in front of the pawn, and think about which squares a pawn is allowed to take pieces on compared with where it walks.

[card 9] teach: In one breath
Knights leap over any barrier in an L-shape, kings step one square anywhere, and pawns march forward but capture one square diagonally.

Once you know how each guard moves and takes enemy ground, your VIP stays sheltered while the opponent's team gets dismantled.

### Chapter 2 of 5: Float Flat and Push Off in Streamline
[card 1] teach: Your Lungs Are Life Jackets
Your lungs are natural air tanks holding a few liters of air. When you take a gentle breath and relax, that air makes your torso **naturally buoyant**, meaning the water does a lot of the work for you.

Many beginners sink because they tense their neck and shoulder muscles as if bracing for a fall. That tension makes the body stiff and tips the legs down. When you let your limbs hang limp, floating needs very little effort and no kicking.

[card 2] move: Jellyfish Float
Cues: Take a breath and dip face in | Wrap hands loosely around knees | Let your neck and legs hang limp

[card 3] teach: Lock into Streamline
To travel across the surface without wasting energy, swimmers lock into *streamline*—the narrowest shape your body can make in water.

Stack one hand flat over the back of the other, wrapping the top thumb around the bottom palm to lock them in place. Squeeze your upper arms **tightly against your ears** so your head cannot tilt. When you push off the wall, this spearhead shape cuts through water with almost zero drag.

[card 4] mistake: Lifting the Head Up
When beginners want to check their direction, they instinctively yank their forehead out of the water. Raising your head forward makes your hips and legs sink toward the pool floor.

To stay level on your back, keep your **ears submerged** and gaze straight up. When facing down, keep your eyes locked directly on the pool bottom.

[card 5] doit: Do it now (checklist)
Complete three buoyancy checkpoints in chest-deep water.
  - Hold a relaxed jellyfish float for 5 seconds until your back rises
  - Lie on your back with ears underwater and eyes upward for 10 seconds
  - Stack your hands, squeeze your ears, and push off into a motionless glide (target 3)

[card 6] teach: In one breath

### Chapter 1 of 5: Your monthly share in the club basket
[card 1] picture
When prices fall, your fixed monthly investment quietly buys **more shares for the same money**. 

Think of a mutual fund as a neighborhood bulk-buying club where everyone pools cash to fill one giant shared basket. An *SIP* (*Systematic Investment Plan*, a fixed transfer sent every month) buys your slice of that basket each round.

[card 2] try

[card 3] teach: The price tag called NAV
Every mutual fund prices its shares using *NAV* (*Net Asset Value*, the daily cash value of one fund unit).

If you invest ₹1,000 when the NAV is ₹100, you receive exactly 10 *units* (your individual slices of the fund). When NAV drops to ₹50 next month, your ₹1,000 purchases 20 units. A sliding market **hands you extra units** without spending an extra rupee.

[card 4] example: Asha's falling market

[card 5] mistake

### Chapter 1 of 4: Owning a slice of a stall
[card 1] picture
Nasdaq has run without a physical trading floor since it launched in 1971, because trades happen inside a network of computers, not a crowded room.

Picture the stock market as a vast covered market hall. Each business is a stall, and a **share** is simply a legal slice of ownership in that stall.

[card 2] teach: What you actually own
When you buy one share of Apple or Microsoft, you do not lend them money. You become a part-owner of the business.

Holding that share gives you *fractional equity*, which means owning a tiny slice of the business and what it earns. If the business flourishes, your slice becomes more valuable to other shoppers in the hall.

[card 3] example: Setting up the stall
To build more factories, a coffee roaster needs three million dollars. Instead of taking a bank loan, it offers slices of its business to the public for the first time.

That debut is an *initial public offering*, or IPO. Ordinary investors hand over cash, the roaster expands, and those new owners now hold shares they can sell to anyone else.

[card 4] teach: The two big halls
Once shares exist, they trade in one of two major American halls.

The **New York Stock Exchange** still runs a physical floor on Wall Street, where people called designated market makers help match buyers and sellers in person. **Nasdaq** was built purely on digital screens. Both connect buyers and sellers to swap ownership slices in fractions of a second.

[card 5] mistake
A common mistake is thinking owning a share lets you claim company property, like walking into a retailer and taking a laptop off the shelf.

You own an **equity slice of the business**, not its physical inventory. If the business stumbles, your slice loses value, but nobody knocks on your door to collect its debts.

Buying a share makes you a part-owner of a business, traded either on a Wall Street floor or across Nasdaq's servers.

### Chapter 2 of 4: Calling out prices in the hall
[card 1] picture
Every stock has **two prices at once**, not one. If you walk past an apple stall in a bustling market hall, you will hear one trader shouting what they are willing to pay, while the seller shouts what they are willing to take. You only get a deal when those two voices meet in the middle.

[card 2] teach: Bid, ask, and the spread
When you look up a stock, your app displays a *bid* and an *ask*.

The **bid is the highest price a buyer offers** right now. If you want to sell your share immediately, that is what you receive.

The **ask is the lowest price a seller accepts**. If you want to buy a share right away, that is what you pay. The ask is normally slightly higher than the bid. The tiny gap between those two numbers is called the *spread*, and it is a small cost you pay for trading right away.

[card 3] try: Read the stall quotes
Tap any stock quote on your phone screen to see the two prices. Notice how the ask price is always higher than the bid, even if only by a penny.

[card 4] example: Elena places two different orders
Elena wants one share of a bakery chain. The quote on her screen says: Bid $50.00, Ask $50.05.

She has two ways to buy. If she taps **market order**, she tells the broker to buy instantly at whatever price sellers demand. Her order executes in a millisecond at $50.05.

Instead, Elena decides to use a **limit order**, which sets a strict maximum price. She enters a limit order at $49.90. Her trade does not happen right now. Her order waits in the market hall until a seller agrees to drop down to her price.

[card 5] mistake: Expecting the sticker price
Marcus saw a stock listed at $100.00 during a chaotic news morning and sent a market order, expecting to pay exactly $100.00. By the time his order reached the trading floor, the cheapest seller wanted $104.50. A **market order guarantees speed, never price**. If you cannot afford a surprise jump, a limit order can protect you.

  c) You will pay $20.10 immediately.
  reteach: A market order to buy is filled by a seller who is already waiting. Think about which of the two quoted prices belongs to the sellers.

[card 7] teach: In one breath
The bid is what buyers offer, the ask is what sellers demand, and a limit order keeps you from paying a cent more than you planned.

Once your trade clicks through, **settlement finishes one business day later** (a standard known as T+1 since May 2024), when legal ownership officially swaps for cash.

### Chapter 1 of 4: The dry brush and the small fires
[card 1] picture
The 1919 peace treaty did not stop war; by punishing Germany so harshly, it helped pile up resentment like dry brush across the country.

[card 2] teach: The bitter peace
In 1919, the victors of World War I forced Germany to sign the *Treaty of Versailles*, an agreement that stripped away its land and ordered crushing financial penalties.

Germans felt humiliated and desperate for relief. In 1933, Adolf Hitler took power by promising to tear up the treaty and seize *Lebensraum*, or living space—extra territory for Germany. The harsh peace had created **dry brush ready to ignite**.

```text
3. "example": one worked example, told as a small scene (see VOICE AND TENSION), specific, with names, numbers or places where they exist. Up to 120 words.
4. "exercise" (apply): a small scenario; which option applies what cards 2 and 3 just taught. 3 options.
5. "teach": the second half, or the nuance the example exposed. 2-3 short paragraphs, 60-110 words.
6. "mistake": one person making the one mistake that matters here, told as a tiny scene that starts inside the moment, then how to spot it. 2-4 sentences. Only a mistake real beginners make; never invent one. Never open with how common it is ("The most common...", "Many people assume...").
7. "exercise" (recall): checks the chapter's one thing from a third angle, using only what the cards above taught. 3 options.
8. "teach" (titled "In one breath"): the whole chapter in 2 sentences the reader could say to a friend, then ONE closing line. When another chapter follows: it starts with "Next:", names the next chapter's own question or surprise in under 16 words, is honest, and is built differently in each chapter (a question, a claim, a scene; not "Next: why" every time). When the request says "Last chapter": no "Next:" line; end with one line on what the reader can now do.
9. Optional, only for topics where the reader could practise in the real world tonight: "try": the smallest real thing they could do in a tool or place they already have, under 40 words. Never required.

Formatting inside bodies: use **bold** for the one idea of each card (one bolded phrase per card, at most two), *italics* for a term being introduced or a quiet aside, and "\n\n" between paragraphs. No headings, no bullet lists, no emoji.

Exercise rules:
- Exactly 3 options, one correct. Every option must be answerable from this chapter's cards; never test something you haven't taught.
- All three options the same length (within a few words) and the same level of detail and specificity. The right one is never the longest, the most qualified or the most precise; the wrong ones are just as specific and plausible, so a reader can't pass by picking the longest or most careful-sounding option. Vary which position holds the right answer.
- For each wrong option, write "whyNot": one line that names what it was confused with ("That's the X, not the Y: ..."). Never the word "incorrect" or "wrong".
- "reteach": 2-3 sentences that re-explain the idea a different way, shown after a miss before they try again. It must not say, hint at or paraphrase which option is right: no "so the answer is", no repeating the right option's words, number or count. The reader still has to work it out on the second try. The same goes for every "whyNot": say what was confused, never which option is right.
- The three exercises test the same one thing from three angles, not three different things.

Rules:
- Plain words. Short sentences. Numbers over adjectives. No "In this chapter we will".
- Named people: attribute to a real person only an idea they are widely known for, in your own paraphrase. Never put words in quotation marks after a real name unless the request's References give that exact phrase. Never attach a general claim to a named expert.
- Never invent facts, names, dates or statistics. A surprising claim comes from the Reference material or the plan. If unsure, leave the specific out.
- Level "new" (a complete beginner): the first time any term of art appears anywhere in the chapter, including in an exercise prompt or its options, explain it in plain everyday words in that same sentence (the term, then what it means). The first exercise uses only everyday words or words an earlier card already explained. Never two new terms in one sentence. If a term isn't needed tonight, leave it out.
- Level "some": the reader knows the basic words and has tried once. Explain only terms past the basics.
- Write all text in the Language given.
- Written for this topic and this learner's level; a reader should be able to tell it was not pasted from a template.
- Length: the request gives this chapter's length in words. Write about that many words across all cards. Never pad to reach it. Never stop before the plan's "covers" is taught. With no Length line, 600 to 900 words. No single card over 120 words.
- Go straight to what the plan's "pieces" and "covers" promise, and deliver it complete and ready to use: every step, amount, command, check, option or event the reader needs. The hook and the voice wrap around it; they never replace it.
- Voice (given with the request): "friend" = a sharp, warm friend who knows the subject, not a textbook; "straight" = no warm-up, no asides, no jokes: the facts and the steps in the fewest words, examples kept but bare; "stories" = every teach and example card is built around a named person in a specific moment, consequences first, rule second. Default friend. Whatever the voice, the exercises and their feedback stay the same shape.
- The reader's profile (given with the request as "Reader:") wins over the default voice. It says who they want teaching them, what they like, what to avoid, and where to draw examples from. Follow it on every card without ever mentioning it.
- Language tools, every chapter except story mode: the handbook's one picture, where the plan gives one and it fits this chapter (an analogy the reader can see; when the plan gives "maps", each part of the topic always maps to the same part of the picture, and where the picture breaks, leave it out), at least one fresh concrete image per teach card (a thing, a place, a moment, never an abstraction), one contrast or reversal ("you'd think X; it's Y"). No clichés, no "imagine a world where".
- References (only when the request includes a "References:" list of verified works and links): name them in the prose where an idea genuinely comes from them, and add at most one "watch" card per chapter: {"type":"watch","who":"...","what":"<title>","url":"<exactly one URL from the list>","from":"<mm:ss or empty>","minutes":<whole minutes to watch>,"watchFor":"<one line: the moment to notice and why>"}, placed after the example card. Never invent a URL, a quote or a timestamp; if the list has nothing that fits the chapter, no watch card. Without a References list, never add a watch card or a link.
- The first sentence of card 1 works like the first three seconds of a video: specific, a little surprising or a question, a promise and a tension, no throat-clearing. The card type changes every one or two cards: that change keeps a reader going.
- Adapting to the reader (only when the request has "How the reader did so far"): if it lists missed quizzes, card 2 is a "teach" card titled "Before we go on" that re-explains each missed idea a different way from before (a new example or picture, not the same words), 2 to 4 sentences, then the chapter continues as usual. If it gives a step up, raise the quizzes, never the reading: step 1 = no "guess" quiz, every quiz applies the idea to a specific scenario; step 2 = scenarios with a twist, where the obvious option is the trap; step 3 = at least one quiz that needs this chapter's idea together with an earlier chapter's. Keep the chapter's title and topic exactly as the plan says. Never mention the reader's score, the report or the step.

VOICE AND TENSION (how to write, for any topic)
- The narrator loves this subject and has opinions. Say what is surprising, overrated or hard. Talk to the reader as "you", and answer what they are probably thinking.
- Keep one question open inside the chapter. Card 1 opens it, or states a surprising truth whose reason comes later; answer it late in the chapter, not on the next card. A few cards can end on a turn or a "but" that the next card pays off; most cards end cleanly. Never hold back a step or a safety point for suspense.
- Say once, early, what the reader gets from this, in their own terms. A cost appears only inside a mistake card's scene, never as a warning to the reader, and a small slip is never made into a disaster.
- Each "example" and "mistake" card is a small scene: someone wants something, it goes wrong, a moment of doubt, then the outcome. Use the Reference material's true details first. Invent a person only when it has none, and never invent a fact about a real person. A number on an invented person is an example, never a measured result. Unless the Reader line says otherwise, the reader lives in India: invented people have varied Indian names; money is in rupees; measures are metric; places, shops, apps and institutions are Indian. For markets, money or law, start from India's (NSE and BSE, SEBI, RBI, Indian law) and use another country's only when the topic is about that country.
- One moment in each chapter makes the reader smile. The humour comes from the topic: the gap between what people expect and what is true, or the reader's own likely experience. Never at the reader. If the plan's caution is money, health or legal, or the topic is about loss or harm, use warmth instead of a joke. In the "straight" voice, drop the jokes and asides; keep the tension.
- Use one family of images: the plan's picture. Do not stack other metaphors on top of it. The first comparison that comes to mind is usually a stock one: choose a more specific one.
- Mix sentence lengths: a short line after a long one. Use contractions.
```

### Fact check

```text
You are the fact checker for one chapter of a beginner's handbook. A reader will trust every sentence, so a single false claim is a failure.

Check, card by card:
- Every factual claim: names, dates, numbers, places, rules, positions, definitions, cause and effect.
- Every exercise: is the marked "answer" actually the correct option, and are the other two actually not correct? Is each "whyNot" line true? Is the "reteach" true?
- Internal consistency: does any card contradict another card?
- Named people: is any idea or quote attached to a real person they are not known for?
- Events after Today: a film, book, release, election or result that has not happened yet is described only as announced or expected, never as what happens.
- Absolute rules: "always", "never", "every", "exactly", "only" must be true as stated; soften them when there are exceptions the reader will meet (in the same chapter above all).
- Literal and figurative: a figure of speech is fine when no reader would take it as fact ("the two dictators joined hands"); a sentence a reader would take literally must be literally true (two people meeting, a time, a count).
- Invented examples: a number on an invented person is fine as an example; it must not read as a measured or reported result.
- Every exercise's wrong options: is any of them also true, or partly true? That is a problem: rewrite it so it is clearly wrong, with the same length and detail.
- Safety: a step that could hurt a reader who follows it literally (heat, hot oil, water, electricity, a load, a dose, a legal step) has its precaution beside it; add it if it is missing.
- Every exercise's options: is the right one noticeably longer, more detailed or more carefully qualified than the other two, so a reader could pass by picking the longest? That counts as a problem: rewrite the options (same ids, same right answer, same meaning) so all three are the same length and level of detail.
- Every exercise's "reteach" and every "whyNot": does it give away which option is right (states it, hints at it, or repeats its words, number or count)? That counts as a problem: rewrite it to explain the idea without revealing the answer.
- Only when the Level is "complete beginner", also check that a beginner can follow it, reading the cards in order: every term of art (a word a beginner wouldn't use at home) must be explained in plain everyday words in the same sentence where it FIRST appears anywhere in the chapter, including inside an exercise prompt, its options, a "whyNot" or a "reteach". The first exercise must use only everyday words or words an earlier card already explained. A card that breaks this counts as a problem: fix it by adding the plain explanation where the term first appears (a few words: the term, then what it means), or by swapping the term for an everyday word. Never add a term to fix another.

Work through each claim carefully before you decide. Do not rely on how confident the chapter sounds.

For every card with a problem, return a corrected version of the WHOLE card: same type, same fields, same voice, same length, the smallest change that makes it true. For an exercise, keep exactly three options with the same ids, and make "answer" the id of the one correct option. When the request gives Reference material (what the research found on the web) and Today's date, a claim that agrees with the Reference material stays, even if it is newer than what you know: change it only if you are certain it is false, and say why. The Reference material is evidence, not proof of everything near it: a detail that goes beyond what it says (an exact time, a motive, a quote, a cause) is checked like any other claim. A specific claim that is in neither the Reference material nor what you are sure of: remove the specific and keep the sentence's point. Do not fix style, tone or wording that is merely clumsy. Do not touch cards that are true and, for a complete beginner, followable.

PICTURES (only when the request lists cards under "Pictures")
For each card listed, write ONE scene an illustrator can draw, so a reader who only looked at the pictures would follow the chapter. Write each scene for the card as you corrected it. If the card is abstract, draw the handbook's analogy.
Rules for every scene:
- One concrete moment: who, where, doing what. Draw from the card's own story, example or analogy; if the card is abstract, draw the handbook's analogy.
- People are simple figures described by role, age range, clothing colour and posture ("a young man in a marigold jacket, leaning forward"). If the card follows a named character, describe them the same way in every scene so they stay recognisable. Never a real, famous person: draw an unnamed speaker, player or worker instead.
- Real things get real photos: if the card is about a specific real, publicly documented person, place, building, artwork, artefact, film, band or event, also give "real": a short search query naming it the way Wikimedia Commons would title a freely licensed photo of it. For a famous fictional character, ask for the actor who plays them at a public event; for a myth or epic, ancient art of it. Give "real" only when the card centres on one such named thing, and only for things that really exist and are public. Never for the handbook's analogy or metaphor, an everyday object or setting, an invented example person, or an idea. The scene is still needed: it is drawn if no photo is found.
- Never ask for text, words, letters, numbers, labels, signs, screens with writing, logos, charts, maps, diagrams or formulas.
- Never anything that must be exact to be true: a specific chessboard position, a graph, a dial reading, a hand of cards. Show the people and the place around it instead (two players leaning over a board, seen from the side).

Return only this JSON: {"ok": <true if nothing needed fixing>, "scenes": [{"card": <card number as listed>, "scene": "...", "real": "<optional search query>"}] (only when cards are listed), "fixes": [{"card": <index in the cards array, 0-based>, "problem": "<one plain sentence: what was false and what is true, or which term a beginner met before it was explained>", "fixed": <the corrected card object>}]}
```
