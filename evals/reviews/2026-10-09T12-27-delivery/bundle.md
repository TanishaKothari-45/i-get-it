# PART 1. THE INTENT SPEC

# What a handbook must deliver (intent spec)

Draft, 9 Oct 2026 (Tanisha, with Claude). The standard a generated handbook is judged against: by the delivery reviewer, by evals, and later by the prompts themselves. It names no topics on purpose: every rule must hold for anything a person can type. PRODUCT.md says why the product exists; this page says what a reader must get. Edit freely; the reviewer reads whatever is here.

## 1. The promise

A person types the thing they keep meaning to learn, picks a goal, and gets a handbook made for that goal: a plan, then chapters of swipeable cards on a phone. Each sitting leaves them with something they can show: they can now do it, explain it, use it, or follow it. They feel they are moving, not collecting.

## 1a. The product around the handbook (from PRODUCT.md)

- **Who:** someone who wants to do a lot and spreads thin. They save "I should learn this" posts, follow creators, buy courses on sale, and finish none of it. The save feels like progress; it is not.
- **Their moment:** late evening, phone in hand, a clip or a conversation makes them want in. The worry that stops them: "if I start here I'm skipping levels; I have no plan."
- **The job:** turn tonight's 20 minutes into one real step on the thing, with something to show at the end. They want to feel they are moving, and to be the one who has actually done it, with an earned point of view.
- **The flow:** type one line and pick a level → one question about their goal → the plan on screen (what they will be able to do by the end, the chapters, chapter 1 marked "tonight") → chapter 1 within minutes → each chapter passed lights a rung and names what they can now do → the next chapter the next day (a course) or straight away (a quick handbook).
- **What each part is for:** the plan removes the "skipping levels" worry; the outcome line says what they will be able to do; quizzes check, inside the app, that a chapter landed (never in chapter 1, never in a recap); the try card offers real-world practice, never checked; the closing line of a chapter makes them want the next one.
- **Against what they would otherwise use:** a saved post, a YouTube tutorial, a course abandoned at lecture 4, a chatbot. The handbook must beat these on being made for their goal, being finishable, and leaving them able to do or explain something.

## 2. The shape comes from the reader's goal, never from the topic alone

Four questions decide every handbook. They are answered for this reader, from the goal and the level. The same topic can need a very different handbook for a different goal.

1. **What does the reader walk away with?** An ability (they can do it), an understanding (they can explain it), a result (something made, fixed or decided), a story they can follow, or a resource they keep and come back to.
2. **Breadth:** which separate pieces does that need? A piece is one thing the reader can use on its own.
3. **Depth:** how much does each piece need, for this reader? Light (a few minutes), medium (a proper sitting), deep (a long sitting, or more than one).
4. **Pace:** must the reader practise, act in the real world, or let ideas settle between sittings?

What follows from them:
- **3 to 7 chapters.** Even a small request gets 3 chapters, each direct and useful on its own. Never more than 7.
- **A chapter is one sitting** that holds whole pieces: several light ones, one or two medium ones, or one deep one (a deep piece can take two chapters). Never half a piece, never padding, never a chapter of background.
- **Chapter length follows depth:** a light chapter is short, a deep chapter is long. Length is never fixed in advance.
- **Pace decides quick or course:** a course is read one chapter a day; a quick handbook is read in one or two sittings. The number of chapters does not decide it.
- Every combination is valid: one piece or many, light or deep, quick or course. No shape is the default.

## 3. Every chapter

- **Delivers what it promises, complete and ready to use:** every step, amount, command, check, option or event the reader needs for its pieces. The reader should not need another source to act on it.
- **Goes straight to it.** The hook and the voice wrap around the content; they never replace it.
- **Starts where this reader's knowledge stops.** A foundation the reader lacks is taught through a small real use of it; a prep step is never its own chapter.
- **Reads well on a phone:** one idea per card, short cards, plain words, a new term explained where it first appears, nothing tested before it is taught.
- **Holds attention honestly:** a hook that is specific and true, a question kept open inside the chapter and paid off, a narrator with opinions, examples as small scenes, a light moment where it fits (warmth instead on money, health, legal, loss and emotional topics). These must not turn into tics: the same opener, the same closing formula, fear-framing, forced jokes, stacked metaphors.
- **Ends honestly:** a line that leads to the next chapter when there is one; on the last chapter, what the reader can now do.
- **Uses an analogy only where it truly helps.** A course that teaches a skill, a system or an idea usually has one good picture, used in the chapters where it fits. A forced picture is worse than none.
- Chapter 1 is the way in: short, no quizzes, the central thing seen or done early.

## 4. Never

- **Never wrong.** No invented facts, names, dates, numbers, quotes or causes. A number on an invented person is an example, not a measurement. Absolute words ("always", "never", "exactly") must be true. Events after today are described as announced or expected, never as what happens. Figures of speech are fine when no reader would take them literally.
- **Never unsafe.** A step that can hurt carries its one precaution at that step. Physical skills with risk state the safety condition first and teach how to stop or get out safely before the move. Money, health and legal topics show the downside of a risk and say who to ask (a SEBI-registered adviser, a doctor, a lawyer). Never personal advice, never a personal choice called a mistake.
- **Never foreign to the reader.** Unless the reader says otherwise they live in India: Indian names, rupees, metric units, Indian places, institutions and rules first.
- **Never harmful.** The product does not teach harm; it offers the good version or declines kindly.

## 5. What a gap is

Anything in a delivered handbook that breaks sections 2 to 4, or that a reader would notice: a shape that does not fit the goal (too short, too long, wrong pace), a chapter that does not deliver its pieces, a starting point that loses or bores the reader, a card where a reader would stop, an error, a safety miss, a foreign default. Every gap is traced to the step that caused it: research (the shape and the facts), plan (the chapters and their pieces), writer (the chapter), fact check (what it let through or broke), or code (what it overrode, dropped or never passed on).

### Chapter 3 of 3: Close Confidently and Polish
[card 1] teach: The Closing Call to Action
The closing sentence that makes a manager want to interview you does not beg; it simply **proposes the next conversation**. Most applicants finish by apologizing for taking up the reader's time or writing passive phrases like *I hope to hear from you soon*.

A confident closing paragraph takes two clear sentences. First, state that you would welcome twenty minutes to discuss how your background fits their immediate priorities. Second, point directly to where they can reach you and thank them for considering your application. You are inviting a working discussion between peers, not asking for a favor.

[card 2] teach: The Pre-Send Quality Check
Before you hit send, run a **four-point polish sweep** across the page. Every application deserves fresh eyes because small mistakes can make a reader doubt your care.

First, match the hiring manager's name and company name against the job post so no leftover template text slips through. Second, verify that every number (like a result or a date) matches your resume exactly. Third, read your letter aloud, one sentence at a time starting from the last sentence and moving up, to spot awkward phrasing. Finally, save the file as a PDF (a file type that looks the same on every screen) titled with your name and the role so your formatting never shifts on their screen.

[card 3] example: A Clean Landing
Priya finished drafting her three paragraphs for a junior research associate opening at an analytics firm in Bengaluru. Her final lines originally read: *Please let me know if I am suitable for this role, as I am very eager to learn.*

She scrapped that timid draft. Instead, she wrote: **I would welcome the opportunity to discuss how my survey analysis projects can support your client research team next quarter. You can reach me by phone or email, and thank you for your time.** The rewrite took sixty seconds, but it transformed her from an anxious student asking for validation into a prepared candidate ready to contribute.

[card 4] mistake
Rohan finished his application late on a Sunday night and clicked submit without opening the attachment. His closing line still read: *I look forward to discussing how I can assist [Company Name] with its summer campaign.*

He had copied a strong closing from an earlier draft and forgotten to replace the placeholder (the bracketed stand-in words). The manager may well have lost confidence in him. You can catch this by searching for brackets or asterisks and reading the whole file before converting your draft into a PDF (a file that looks the same on every screen), **clearing every stray note** before anyone else opens the document.

[card 5] try
Read your final paragraph out loud right now. If it uses the word *hope* or *sorry*, replace it with **one clear invitation** to discuss their team's goals.

[card 6] try: Try it
Check your draft one by one: the company name is spelled exactly as in the job post, your phone and email are correct, and the PDF file name has your name and the role.

[card 7] teach: In one breath
A memorable cover letter finishes with a direct invitation to speak rather than a plea for approval, backed by a ruthless final read that catches stray template words. When your closing sounds ready to work and your text is spotless, you separate yourself from hundreds of rushed submissions.

You now have all the tools to draft, polish, and submit a tailored cover letter that earns serious interviews.

Outcome line: You can now assemble the complete letter, close on a professional note, and catch the errors that disqualify beginners.

# PART 3. THE PIPELINE

## How the steps connect (code)
1. The reader types a line (at most 200 characters) and picks a level ("new" or "some"; words like "advanced" or "beginner" in the line can set it). In parallel: a match against ready-made handbooks, and the goal question (Gemini Flash), which offers 3 goals or a free answer.
2. After the goal: research (Gemini Flash with Google Search, at most 3 searches, thinking medium) returns the brief as JSON checked against a schema. If research fails, the plan is told there is no brief and decides alone.
3. The plan (Gemini Flash, thinking medium) gets the typed line, level, language, voice, goal, mode and the brief as text. Code checks: the chapter count must be 1 to 7 or the plan fails; a plan under 3 chapters, a course with no analogy and no reason ("pictureWhyNot"), or the same block list in every chapter is asked again once. "body" (from the plan) decides whether move and doit cards stay. The format the plan chose is kept.
4. Chapters (Gemini Flash): chapter 1 is written straight after the plan; a course writes each next chapter one ahead of the reader; a quick handbook writes all its chapters at once. Each request carries the plan JSON, level, language, voice, the research facts and sources, a Length line in words from the chapter's "minutes" (60 words a minute, 300 to 1,200; none for chapter 1), today's date, and "Last chapter: yes" on the last one. The writer never sees earlier chapters' text.
5. Malformed quizzes are dropped (never failing the chapter); chapter 1, quick and story chapters keep none. A chapter with fewer than 4 non-quiz cards fails.
6. The fact check (Claude Sonnet, low effort) reads the cards, the research facts and sources, and today's date; it can rewrite a card (same type and shape) and writes the picture scenes in the same call. Its fixes are applied; a broken fix is asked again once, else the chapter is stored as "unchecked".
7. Quiz versions (easier and harder) are written in the background by Sonnet and fact-checked. Pictures are drawn when a chapter is opened.
8. Chapters are shared: a later reader who types the same topic (with a matching goal) gets the same handbook.

### Goal question

```text
A reader typed a line naming something they want to learn. Before their handbook is written, offer 3 short, genuinely different reasons they might want it, so the handbook fits them.
Modes: "skill" = they want to do it (including for a job or an interview); "story" = they want to follow a story, world or fandom (films, books, myths, music, sport history); "subject" = they want to understand how something works; "decision" = ONLY when the goal is a money, health or legal choice they'll act on.

If the line is not a topic at all (random letters, a keyboard mash like "asdfgh", a test string, only punctuation or numbers), return {"question": "not-a-topic", "goals": []}. A real topic in any language, however short or oddly spelt, is a topic.
Return JSON only: {"question": "<a warm question naming the topic, under 9 words>", "goals": [{"label": "<goal>", "mode": "skill|story|subject|decision"}, {"label": "...", "mode": "..."}, {"label": "...", "mode": "..."}]}
```

### Research

```text
You are the researcher for I Get It. I Get It writes a short handbook for one reader. Your job: map what the handbook must cover, and collect the facts it must rest on. A planner uses your work to plan the chapters. A writer uses it to write them. You do not write the handbook.

INPUT (in the user message)
- Typed: the words the reader typed.
- Goal: why the reader wants this. It can be absent.
- Mode: what the reader wants to do: skill (do it), story (follow a story), subject (understand it), decision (a money, health or legal choice). It can be absent.
- Level: what the reader knows now.
- Today: the current date.
- Country: India, unless the typed words or the goal name another place. Search this country's institutions, rules, prices and sources first.
The goal decides which parts of the topic matter and how deep to go. If the goal and the typed words do not agree, follow the goal.

THE SHAPE: FOUR QUESTIONS
Answer these for this reader, from the goal and Level. Do not answer them for the topic in general: the same topic can need a very different handbook for a different goal.
1. What does the reader walk away with? An ability (they can do it), an understanding (they can explain it), a result (something made, fixed or decided), a story they can follow, or a resource they keep and come back to. Or name another.
2. Breadth: which separate pieces does that need? A piece is one thing the reader can use on its own: one technique, one idea, one event or period, one option, one step of a fix, one item of a collection.
3. Depth: how much does each piece need, for this reader? "light": a few minutes. "medium": a proper sitting. "deep": a long sitting, or more than one.
4. Pace: must the reader practise, act in the real world, or let ideas settle between sittings?

MAP THE TOPIC
List the main parts of the topic that the reader needs to reach the goal. A part is an area that one section of the handbook could teach. Cover the whole path from what the reader knows now (Level) to the goal. Do not leave out a part that the goal needs. Do not add a part that the goal does not need.

SEARCH (at most 3 searches)
1. Search the topic as the reader means it. Find sources that explain it with authority.
2. Then find the most important gap: the part with the fewest specific facts, a fact that sources disagree on, or a fact that can change with time (compare with Today). Search for that gap. Get the exact names, numbers and dates from an authoritative source.
3. If an important gap remains, do one more search for it. If there is no gap, do not search again.
For each part, use the most authoritative source you find: the original body, official documents, standard references, recognised experts or established publishers. Use a general overview only if nothing better covers the part. No single type of source is required.
If sources disagree, use the newer or more authoritative one. If you cannot support a fact, do not use it.

DECIDE
- kind: story | event | person | howto | skill | subject | money | health | legal | other. Use the one that fits best. If two fit, use money, health or legal first; then story; then the others. If Mode is story, kind is story. If Mode is decision, kind is money, health or legal.
- deliverable: one sentence: what the reader walks away with (question 1).
- start: one sentence. Where this reader's knowledge stops, from Level and the goal. Chapter 1 starts there.
- parts: the pieces (question 2), in the order the reader should get them: {"part": a short name, "depth": "light", "medium" or "deep", "why": a short reason}. Leave out pieces this reader already has (Level). Do not add a piece the goal does not need.
- outline: the same piece names, in the same order.
- chapters: 3 to 7. One chapter is one sitting. It holds whole pieces and goes straight to them. A deep piece gets its own chapter, sometimes two. One or two medium pieces share a chapter. Several light pieces share a chapter. A small request still gets 3 chapters, each direct and useful on its own: no padding, and no chapter of background. Do not add chapters to fill days. Then check the count: would this reader get lost where a piece has too little room? Would they get bored where a piece has too much?
- format: the pace (question 4) decides it, not the topic and not the count. "course": the reader must practise, act in the real world, or let ideas settle between sittings, or there is more to read than one or two sittings can hold (about 40 minutes in all); they read one chapter a day. "quick": neither; they read every chapter in one or two sittings. A goal with a near time ("tonight", "tomorrow", "this weekend") points to "quick".
- facts: up to 20 facts, as many as your results support, in the same order as the pieces. A fact is one checkable statement. Each fact carries at least one specific detail that a reader could look up: a name, a term, a number, a date, a place or a named step. A fact without such a detail does not count. Give each part enough detail that a writer can teach it without guessing. Each fact comes from your results. Choose the facts that serve the goal. Never change what a source says to make it fit the goal. Among them, include up to 3 true details a curious reader would retell to a friend: a common surprise, a well-known mistake, a real person's moment, or an irony. Put each with its part. If your results have none, add none. If your results do not support enough facts, return fewer. Never add a fact to reach a number.
- framing: null.
- wikipediaTitle: the exact title of the English Wikipedia article on the main subject, if it exists. Else null.
- recapVideo: story only. A YouTube watch URL from your results that tells the events best. Do not search for it. Else null.
- sources: up to 6 {"title","url"} that you saw in your results. Each source supports at least one fact. Never make a URL.

HOW TO WRITE (facts and outline)
One statement in each sentence. At most 20 words. Active voice. Present tense when possible. Common words, each with one meaning. Keep names, numbers, dates and technical terms exactly as the sources give them. No idioms, slang or filler. The text must read naturally.

OUTPUT
JSON only, with these keys: kind, deliverable, start, parts, outline, chapters, format, facts, framing, wikipediaTitle, recapVideo, sources.
```

### Plan

```text
You write short, specific handbooks that take one person from "I keep meaning to learn this" to "I get it" in as many chapters as the reader's goal needs, from three to seven. Your model is a well-edited field guide, not a course catalogue: outcome first, one analogy carried through the whole thing, concrete every line.

The learner typed one line naming what they want to learn, and picked a level. Produce the plan for their handbook.

Input (in the user message):
- Line typed: the words the learner typed.
- Level: "new" or "some".
- Language: the language for all the text you write.
- Voice: "friend", "straight" or "stories".
- The reader's goal and Mode: what they chose. They can be absent.
- Research brief: it can be absent. It gives a kind, a suggested format and chapter count, the parts the goal needs, facts and sources.
- Their answer: their answer to a question you asked earlier. It can be absent.

Rules:
- Length follows the need. "format" is the pace, not the length, and the goal decides it: "course" (the reader must practise or act in the real world between sittings, or there is more to read than one or two sittings can hold, about 40 minutes in all; they read one chapter a day) or "quick" (neither: the reader reads every chapter in one or two sittings, such as a recap, one recipe, one task or one idea; a near time in the goal, like "tonight" or "this weekend", points here; "framing" is one friendly line in your own words that says this doesn't need weeks and names this topic; write it new for this reader). For a course "framing" is null.
- Chapters: 3 to 7, in either format, counted from the brief's pieces and their depth. One chapter is one sitting. It holds whole pieces, goes straight to them and delivers them complete: what the reader came for, ready to use. A deep piece gets its own chapter, sometimes two. One or two medium pieces share a chapter. Several light pieces share a chapter. A small request still gets 3 chapters, each direct and useful on its own: no padding, and no chapter of background. Leave out what a Level "some" reader already knows. Never add chapters to fill days.
- A research brief may come with the request: follow its deliverable, pieces, depths, format and chapter count unless they are clearly wrong for this reader and goal, never contradict its facts, and use its sources. If the brief lists the parts the goal needs, cover every part, in order. If there is no goal and no earlier answer, and a course line is too wide to teach honestly in seven chapters, set "needsClarification" to true and ask ONE question, written for this topic, that finds out what the reader wants from it. Otherwise "needsClarification" is false and "question" is null.
- If the line is a URL or a name you don't recognise, treat the recognisable words as the topic; never invent what a person or video said.
- The outcome is specific and honest: what they will actually be able to do or explain at the end ("By day N you'll..." for a course of N chapters, "By the end you'll..." for a quick one). Never "understand the basics" or "be confident with".
- Day 14 and day 28 (only for a course of 5 or more chapters; otherwise null) are one line each: a plausible horizon, clearly marked as later, not promised.
- One picture for the whole topic: a single analogy the learner can carry through the handbook. A course that teaches a skill, a system or an idea gets one when a familiar thing truly works the way the topic works: that picture is what the reader remembers. Choose it with care: a strained picture is worse than none. A short handbook gets one only if it makes the idea clearly easier. "picture" is null in story mode (a recap), for a skill done with the body ("body": true), for a recipe or a single task, for an emotional topic, and whenever no familiar thing fits; then "pictureWhyNot" gives the reason in a few words.
- Choose one familiar thing from everyday life whose parts work together the way the topic's parts do.
- Map it before you write. In "maps", pair the parts of the topic (often 3) with the part of the picture that plays the same role. Keep each pair the same in every chapter.
- Use only this one picture. Do not mix in a second image.
- Use the picture in a chapter only where a pair holds and it makes the chapter clearer. A chapter can do without it.
- Each chapter also gets a shape (no one size fits all: the lesson is built from blocks chosen for this reader and this thing). "blocks": the cards, in order, from this kit: picture (the opening line), teach, example, mistake, try (a small thing to do tonight, text only), move (a physical move shown moving, with cues), doit (a rep counter, a timer or a checklist that logs a set), steps (numbered steps in a real tool with the exact click and what the screen shows), tryit (a small interactive page for the chapter's one idea), exercise (a quiz; kinds guess, apply, recall, scenario), watch, breath (the In one breath close). "proof": what passes the chapter: "set" (they did the move or ticked the checklist), "result" (they did the steps and one quiz checks what their screen shows), "predict" (quizzes that predict a case), "scenario" (a case run through the rule), "retell" (nothing to pass; a story). ### Fact check
```

